Mr. Chairman, Governor, and you, my fellow citizens:  It is a real pleasure to have the chance of addressing you here in your [-bxxxx-] beautiful square this evening.   I thank you all for your greeting, and I know that the rest of you will not grudge [-xx-] my saying that I thank particularly those who have done me the honor to turn out as a guard for me - the veterans of the great war (Applause).   I am always glad to see the veterans anywhere, but I like particularly to see them in any educational center, because if it comes down to bedrock facts, what I preach is just what you have practiced. (Laughter and applause).   Practicing will beat preaching every day of the week, even as preaching.   Practice is the best kind of preaching.   The best way you can preach is to practice by example.

This is the city with the second oldest normal school in the country, and the president of the Alumni Association of that school is with me here - my Secretary, Mr. Cortelyou (Applause).   I do not speak of him, of his services and his ability, merely because if I spoke of them at all I should speak of them truthfully, and it would be unpleasant for him to have me use the language practically of flattery which I should have to use (Applause).

Now our republic has as one of its cornerstones the education of the citizen.   Education is not all.   The educated scamp is a scamp still and all the more {dengerous|dangerous} to the {comkunity|community}.   But admitting that, it is always true that while education is not all, without it we would not amount to much. We must have a high degree of education in the average citizen or we are not going to be able to solve aright the great problems {pre sented|presented} to us. It is no small honor, no small distinction, for a town to have within its borders the second oldest of the {instututions|institutions} which have accomplished such a {mervelous|marvelous} work in giving higher scholastic training to those best fitted to receive it.   Our public school system is broad at the base, but goes upwards so that those who wish are able to get the highest type of education. Our public school system, it is a mere truism to say, stands at the foundation of good citizenship;   It is one of the component parts .  There is no

[pb 157 424:0635]

one stone that makes up all the foundation.   Education in the schools is one, but it is not a substitute for education at home.   Let no father and mother lay to their souls the flattering unction that they can shirk their duties and think that those duties will be performed by the school teacher, no matter how good that teacher  is.   All of you know an occasional father or mother who does just that thing.   We have to have the education; we must have the home bringing up; we must have the trained mind; and then we must have, in addition, training for what is more than mind - training for character.   That is fundamentally what counts in the life of the citizen and in the life of the state.   To get the best results we must have a high degree of education, but the highest degree of education, if unaccompanied by the development in the man's moral side which produces character, will avail but little. Character [-x-] [+is+] that combination of virtues which include the milder virtues and also the sterner - which makes a man a good man and also a strong man   . Now you, the men of the great war - you could have done nothing, you would have done nothing, if you had not felt in your souls the fire of love of country, of unflinching resolution to keep the flag from being [-xxxxxx-] torn asunder, if you had not deep in your souls felt the impulse of patriotism.   But you had to have more than that.   It was necessary to be patriotic, but patriotism did not help the man who had a tendency to run away.   I do not care what excellent, amiable qualities a man has, even if a {goood|good} man in his home relations, devoted to his country, and all that, if he does not have the rugged qualities of courage and hardihood in him he will make but a poor fist of being a good soldier (Applause).   You want the man to be a patriotic man.   That had to be at the foundation of the man's character. But you {ndeeded|needed} other qualities  in him.   You needed the qualities of iron endurance, of resolution, of determination to see the thing through.   You needed the qualities of sound common sense - that is another quality that is necessary.   You recollect in the early days before Bull Run how most of the people who had stayed at home were perfectly bent upon having those that had gone to the front go to  Richmond at once (Laughter).   They said that Abraham Lincoln was not doing his duty because he had not gone to Richmond at once. Then came Bull Run, and the very people who a {weekbefore|week before} had been saying [pb 158 424:0636] that the delay was shameful, that the army must advance and enter Richmond, now said that the war was over, that defeat must be acknowledged, that the war was over.   But you didn't look at it that way (Laughter).   You stayed there and the war lasted for three years and nine months, and when it got over it was over the other way (Laughter and applause).   Well, the reason you won was you were not of a hysterical nature.   I do not like hysteria, especially in a grown man.   There are plenty of evils, any number of them.   Not one of them will be cured by hysterics.   The men that counted in the civil war were the men who did not demand the impossible or attempt the impossible, but the men who were bent on seeing that everything possible was done.   And so in civic life.   The good citizen is the man who does what can be done as well as it possibly can be done.   He does not expect to {anhieve|achieve} the millenium - the millenium is a good way off yet.   But he does believe that something can be done to further the reign of righteousness on this earth.   Abraham Lincoln said, do the best possible, and if it is impossible to do the best, do the best possible.   The reason that we got through the civil war as we did was because the Giver of Good had put at the head of the nation in the time of the nation's supreme peril, Abraham Lincoln (Great applause)[-xx-] Abraham Lincoln, said, patient, kindly, with his unerring common sense, with his resolute purpose to do all that could be done, and his refusal to waste his strength in attempting or to be guilty of the baseness of promising what could not be done - who for four years worked and suffered for the people, staggering under as heavy a load as ever mortal man bore, and then in the supreme hour of the nation's triumph shed his own blood on the alter upon which the blood of the bravest and best of the young men of the people had already been poured.

Now to be a good citizen and to get good government, you need three qualities - sometimes others, but always these three.   In the first place, you need honesty, using the word in its widest signification - decency, square dealing as between man and man - the quality that makes a man a good husband, a good father, a good neighbor, and that makes him do his duty  by the state also.   If a man has not got righteousness and decency in him, then the abler he is the more dangerous he is.     You have got to have decency and morality first.   But that is not enough.   The moral man who is

[pb 159 424:0636]

afraid is of mighty little use in the world.   You must have with morality and decency, courage - courage that will dare in battle - courage that will dare in civic life - courage that endures - and the courage that goes forward both.   Courage and honesty even will not {avaul|avail} to save a man who was born foolish.   In order to get good citizenship the average man must have honesty, he must have courage, and [-[?h?]-] united to them he must have the saving grace of common sense (Cheers and applause).
