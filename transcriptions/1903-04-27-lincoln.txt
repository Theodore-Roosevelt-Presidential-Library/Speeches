Congressman Burkett, and you my fellow-Americans, my fellow-citizens:

It is indeed a pleasure for me to come to the state of Nebraska, and now to come to its capital [-[illegible]-]. In thanking all of you for your greeting, the rest I am sure will not mind my saying a special word of thanks to the men to whom we owe it that there is a [-xxxxxxxx xxxxxxxxxxxxx-] United States or a President of it to-day.(Cheers and applause.) I come here now at the outset of the 20th century because from '61 to '65 you had it in you to be willing to give all, life itself, for the sake of the ideal-for the ideal of the Union, and of freedom. And it was natural enough that when in '98 the little war came Nebraska should have sent her sons to the Philippines to show that the spirit that had burned in their sires[+'+] hearts was not extinguished yet. I greet you. Let me say a word also to the representatives of the University.(Cheers and applause.) I am glad to see you. My creed is a fairly simple one, whether for boy or man. I believein play, and I believe in work. Play hard [-xxxxx-] while you play, and when you work do not play but work hard.(Cheers and applause.) I also wish to say a word of special greeting to two other bodies. First, to the National Guard. I wish to congratulate your city upon the admirable arrangements, and to say how much I appreciate the Guard having turned out[-xxxxxxxxxx-] and the way they carried themselves, the ease with which they made it possible for this great audience to be reached. Now a special word to and about the {schoal|school} children. Coming through Nebraska to-day I have rejoiced in your prosperity; I rejoice in your fertile soil; I rejoice in the crops you raise, but after all the best product of any soil is the product of the men and women. I am mighty glad to see your children. I think you have a good stock an[+d+] I want to see it go on.

I have but a word to say to you. I wish to speak one word only upon government-good gover nment. There is nothing peculiar or wonderful about getting good government anymore than there is anything peculiar or wonderful in any man's making a success in private life .

[pb 129 425:0290]

The same qualities that make a good man in hi s family, a good husband and father, a good neighbor, a man with whom you like to work or to deal, these same qualities make him a good citizen, a good man in the state when applied in his relations to the state. We need honesty, we need courage, we need common sense. We need to show in civil life the same [-xxxxxxxx-] spirit that you showed in the Civil [?War?] in battle. What you cared to know about as to the man on your right and on your left was not the way in which he worshipped his maker, not his social standing or wealth; you cared nothing whether he were a farmer or a mechanic, a lawyer or a business man, bricklayer or banker; what you wanted to know was whether he would do his duty like a man; that is what you cared for; whether he would stay put when the time came.(Applause.) It is the same thing in civil life now. Shame to any man who treats [-xx-] another with arrogance because he is less well-off, or with envy and hatred and malice because he is better off. Either feeling is a contemptible feeling for an Americ an [-x-] freeman to have.

We can get on in this republic only if we act on the theory of treating each man on his worth as a man, [-xx-] guaranteeing him his rights, and seeing that he does no wrong, [-[illegible]-] and that he is protected in return in so far as he doe s right. Our land has gone on because we enjoy liberty under the law; and it will go on prosperously just so long as capitalist and wage-worker, the man of the farm and the man of the town all al ike proceed in that spirit. If we allow envy, hatred, anger, to rule us; if we permit wrong to be done by any man against another; if we strive to interfere with the just rights of any man or[-x-] fail to protect him in them, by just so much are we coming short of the standard set for us by the[-x-] men who in [?'76?] founded this nation, by the men who in the years from '61 to '65 preserved it. Anything that I say of that kind must necessarily be general in its nature. Each community has its own special conditions to which the rule applies; but the principles of which I have spoken must be applied in each individual case according to that case[+'+]s need, under penalty of seeing partial failure at least-the principles of order, of law, of liberty under and through and by the law[+,+] {hot|not} in spite of it, under an d through and by it[+—+]The spirit of genuine brotherhood,

[pb 130 425:0290]

the spirit which looks to one[+'+]s neighbor's interests as well as one[+'+]s own, and which thinks it shame to impugn the ri ght of any one else. More than that; something can be done by the law; something can be done by the [-[illegible]-][+honest+], and fearless, and wise administration of the law; but after all, in the last resort, we must depend upon the high average of our individual citizenship for success. Uniform in battle is a good thing; a good rifle is a better thing; but if you get a poor man with a good rifle he will be beaten by a good man with a club. (Applause) You have got to have the proper spirit in the man behind the law, in the average citizen, to get the proper results from the law. In the last resort it is upon the average of our citizenship that we have to rely. More than that; we can do a good deal by combination among ourselves, combination for business purposes, combination to help those who are wageworkers, always provided that each combination acts under and in accordance with the law; always provided that we can help one another. There is not a man of us here who does not at times stumble, at times slip, at times need a helping hand. Shame to the brother who will not then extend the helping hand. Help any man up; help him to try to walk; but if he lies down you cannot carry him. (Laughter) If he lies down and will not walk, trying to carry him will not help him and it will not help yourself. You can help a man in just one way -- by helping him to help himself. That is the only way [+by+] which in the end you can do a man real good. You can do good that way and each of us is bound to try to do good that way.

Men and women of Nebraska, I congratulate you upon what I see in your great and beautiful state. I believe that whatever may be the temporary ebb and flow, on the whole Nebraska will in the future share to even more than her expected proportion in the prosperity that is sure to come in the end to [-xxx-] a nation such as ours, composed of such people with the continent back of them. I believe in it because I believe in the character of the men and women who make up the state here. I believe in it, as I believe in the future of the United States, because I believe in the average citizen of the United States. It is a great thing to have the soil that you have got in this state , to have the crops that you have got. You need them as the foundation upon which to build;

[pb 131 425:0291]

but the building itself depends upon the use that you make of that foundation.

I congratulate you upon your universities and public schools. I congratulate you upon your churches and upon what is done in your households. I congratulate you most of all because you are doing your best to keep up the average . of citizenship here. To make a good citizen you need first of all honesty, in its widest sense, the spirit of fair dealing as between man and man, the spirit that makes a man jealous of the rights of his neighbors as he is jealous of his own. You of the great war won because each of you had it in him to care for some thing [+else+] more than for himself, to care for the honor of the regiment, the honor of the flag, the honor of the nation, more even than for his own life. So we will get good citizenship only on condition that we have in us the power of adherence to a lofty ideal; and that is not enough. In the civil war it did not make any difference how patriotic a man was if he ran away. In addition to the love of country you had to have the courage that made you put that love of country into action. I do not care how good a man is, how virtuous he is, if he sits down at home he does {hot|not} count. The man that counts is the decent man, the honorable man, who has got the stuff in him to go out into the world, into the hurlyburly of actual life, and hold his own as a man. among men. That is the man that counts. You have got to have strength and courage, the qualities of virile manhood in addition to the qualities of honesty and {decancy|decency}. And that is not enough. I do not care how honest a man is and how brave he is, if he is a natural born fool you can do little with him. In addition to courage, in addition to honesty, you must have the saving grace of common sense . Shame on the man whos e heart is hard. I want softness of heart, but I do not want it to be extended and to be softness of head. We do not need brilliancy or genius in [?citizenship?]; we need most of all the capacity which makes a man do well the ordinary deeds; we need to develop all the ordinary qualities which we feel the average citizen should possess, the qualities that make a man and that make a good man, the qualities that make a man good in his family, good in relations with his neighbors and a square and a brave man in his dealings with the state, the qualities that stand above physical strength, that stand above intellect, the qualities that go to make up what we call character. In their essence these qualities can be divided into three, the quality of honesty, the quality of co urage, and the quality of common sense.

[pb 132 425:0291]

I thank you for having come to [-xxx-] greet me. It is a pleasure to [?see you?] here. I believe in you with all my heart. I believe in your future; I [?know?] you; I welcome you here to-day, and I bid you Godspeed for the future. (Great applause)
