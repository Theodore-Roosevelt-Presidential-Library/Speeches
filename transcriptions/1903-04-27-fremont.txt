Mr. Mayor; and you, My Fellow Citizens:

It is a great pleasure to greet you to-day. I want to say a word of special thanks to the men of the Grand Army of the Republic who have come forth to greet me this afternoon here as elsewhere. This is the anniversary of Grant's birthday (Applause) and it is because of what he and you and those like you did that we now have a country or a President. It has always seemed to me that the men who fought in the great war and the pioneer who first tamed the rugged wilderness did things that were in their essence similar, and have taught us a lesson which we can take to good account in more ways than one. The life of the pioneer was a hard life. The life in a new country is apt to be hard, but at the end of it comes a triumph that can never be known by those who walk in the soft places. Look back into your own lives, [-xxx-], my fellow citizens, and see if fundamentally it is not true of you what is so preeminently true of the men who fought in the civil war. When you entered to fight between '61 and '65 you left your homes and left the life of ease you did not do it for the money. It was only $13. a month. For that you faced the well-nigh intolerable toil of the day marches under the summer sun, when at about noon one blanket was too heavy to carry and at midnight it was not heavy enough; and when you were raw you attempted to throw the blanket away. You remember the bitter cold of the freezing midwinter watches at night in the trenches. You remember the insufficient food, the fever cots in the hospital, the painful death by bullet or by disease. You risked it all, and gladly, for the sake of fealty to an ideal. And now you hand on to those who are to come after you, to your children and your children's children, to the men of the new generation, as the proudest memories not merely of your lives but of all the history of the nation-- what, the days of ease? No. The days when you toiled and worked and fought, the days when you played your part well as men. That is what you handed on as the proudest of memories. So it is with all of us, my fellow citizens. When we come to look back {inour|in our} lives the times of which we are proudest, the times which make[-s-] us feel that we have a right to [?raise?] our heads {bcase|because} we did our duty well and fairly, {wre|were} the times of toil, [?and distress?], and effort, the times when we showed ourselves men [?for?] to do a man's part in the world. I do not envy the man who, when he has come to the [pb 135 425:0293] end of his career, can say that he has always had life easy. The man who can say that usually can add that his life has not been worth living. I do not envy him, I pity him. I {mak|may} despise him, but I do not envy him.

When we come to bring up the generation that is to come after us we can train them in two ways. We can train them to avoid work and worry, to shirk difficulties, or we can train them to meet them and overcome them. I passed outside there between two great rows of school children. I have been enjoying to the full my trip through Nebraska. I admire your state, its wonderful fertility, its crops, the assured hope that it will go steadily onward in prosperity . I like your crops. I like what you produce, but the best product is the men and the women. That is what counts most. So I was particularly glad to see the children. {Ilike|I like} the stock and I like to see it kept up . We all know among our friends some man, and I am sorry to say sometimes some woman, who deliberately starts to bring up his or her children so that they will be useless in the world, and you find it in every rank of society, among the wealthy and among those of small means. You will see the man of wealth who deliberately and of set purpose lets his sons come up with no idea of there being any duty, any work, to the world, but to lead their lives in that most vapid and most miserable of all pursuits-- the pursuit of pleasure as the sole end of existence. You will see that at one end;[-[illegible]-] and at the other [?ed?] you will see the [-[illegible]-] woman, who, having worked hard, says "Well, I have had to work, my girls they shall be ladies; they shall not work." The mother stays in the kitchen and the girls in the parlor. Or you will see the father who is a hardworker, but lacks the commonsense estimate of his duties as a father and does not make his boys work in their turn, does not teach them as they grow up that they must do their own work. That father, that mother is not kind. That is not kindness. That is weakness of the most foolish type. Nothing can be greater folly than to spare some small present discomfort to the child at the of imperilling all of his or her usefulness and happiness in the future. The boy or the girl who is to be the man or woman of the future , to make up the average of our citizenship, if in him or her the qualities are not developed in youth you will find it difficult to get a good report of them afterwards.

In the civil war you and those like you made good soldiers, I think about the best that the world has seen; you needed training, you needed weapons, but there were some men whom no training and no weapons could do anything for, be-[pb 136 425:0293]cause if the man did not have the {stuffin|stuff in} him you could not get it out of him. You had to have the man with the fighting edge to him in order that you might make a fighting man out of him. So it is in citizenship; we have to have the qualities out of {wich|which} good citizenship is made, even if those qualities are in the raw, in order to develop good citizenship. The law counts. A good great can be done by the law; a good deal can be done by the honest and fearless administration of the law; and shame to any community unless it insists upon having {notonly|not only} just and righteous laws but laws that are construed without respect to the indi[-[illegible]-]viduals[-[illegible]-]. [-[illegible]-] (Applause) We need then a law that will be administered impartially and fairly; that shall be no respector of persons; that shall apply to all alike; that shall guarantee to each man his life, his liberty, and his right to pursue his happiness as he wishes in any way so long as he does not infringe his neighbors rights. But back of the law, back of the administration of the law, must lie the average quality of the individual citizens. Nothing can take the place of that. There are other nations with governments {foundedon|founded on} the same principles as ours, with constitutions not very different from our, which, nevertheless, in their actual working have turned out wholly different from ours, because there was a different quality of citizenship. In the long run nothing can take the [-xxxxx-] place of the man's own individual qualities. We need strong bodies; even more we need strong minds; but more than all we need what stands above body and above mind, that is character; character into which three chief components enter. In the first place, honesty, decency, the spirit of fair dealing, of treating your neighbor as you would wish to be treated yourself. One of the things [+against+] which we all need to keep watch is a certain tendency {whoch|which} we see too often to deify what is called mere smartness. Mere smartness as such if unaccompanied by the moral qualities -- we all of us know the man of whom certain other foolish men say " Well he succeeded; he is dre'ful smart "" and you say "Yes, but he is not straight", they say,"Yes, but he is so smart,"; that kind of smartness is a curse to the community; it breeds the dishonest businessman, the wageworker who shirks his work, the politician who is untrue to his promises and to his oath; that kind of promise, that kind of smartness you cannot afford to condone. You have got to have honesty, and beware of the man who will teach you that he will be dishonest in your interest. The man who will steal from you will steal from you. That is a [?sound?] principle [pb 137 425:0294] upon which to act in public and private life alike. The man who will try to teach you that any form of law-breaking, of violence, of disorder, of avoidance of the law, of cheating, any form of dishonesty or impropriety of conduct is to be condoned because it is in your interest, is a man to be shunned, for {is|if} he will do wrong to help you, if he gets the chance he will do wrong to hurt you. (Great applause)
