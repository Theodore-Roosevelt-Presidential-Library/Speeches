Mr. Chairman; and you, My Fellow Citizens:

I thank you all for greeting me to-day. It is a pleasure to speak in a county commemorating by its name one of those pioneers to whose energy and foresight we owe the existence of this country, just as we owe the fact that we have a country at all and its preservation to the men who fought in the great civil war. Throughout Washington I have been greeted wherever I have gone by men wearing the button which shows that they fought for the country when Abraham Lincoln called to arms; and the qualities which enabled them to render to the nation a service literally inestimable were fund amentally the qualiti es shown by the men who came hither and carved a commonwealth out of the wilderness. The men of the civil war had to show self-abnegation, the willingness to sacrifice all for a lofty ideal, and furthermore the qualiti es of soul and of body that could make that willingness effective; for not only did they have to have the spirit, but they had to have the other qualities which would make the spirit good. The bravest man, the most devoted man, if, for his misfortune, weak, unable to march, unable to fight, could not have rendered the service that you of the civi l war rendered, and so in the times of the pioneers the service had to be rendered by men not only strong of mind, but strong of body and strong of soul. The pioneer days are gone, but the need for the pioneer virtues is just as great as ever. A great nation must inevitably pay the penalty of its greatness by the need for effort just as the man who plays a strong part must inevitably pay the penalty of constant exertion in order that his part may be played well. The {llife|life} of ease, the life of absence of effort, the life where nothing ever threatens or disturbs the man, is the life that is not worth living. (Applause) The life that counts is the life of the man who is willing to take risks when there is a sufficient object to gain, and who is always willing to pay in effort, in labor, in toil, in daring, for an adequate result.. Just exactly as in the times of the civil war the victory could only come to those who were willing if necessary {tp|to} pay the price and who went in with their eyes open knowing there would be a price to pay, so in civic life the prizes must come to those who are willing to pay in toil and in effort for them. Just so it is with the nation. A small and stationary nation can afford not to take risks, can afford not to try to do great deeds, and can hope to escape the necessity for solving problems of weighty moment. But we are not a small or stationary nation. We belong to a nation which we hold now to
[pb 111 425:0481] be the greatest upon earth and we are bound to make its greatness grow steadily as the years pass, and we would not be contented for one moment to purchase immunity from every risk within and without at the cost of stopping to go forward, of stopping to count in the world. We have got a big part to play; we cannot help playing it; all we can decide is whether we will play it well or play it badly. I know you too well my fellow count rymen [-xx-] to have any doubts as to what your decision will be. (Applause)

The [-tremendous-][+complex+] industrial growth which has brought in its train so much that is good has of necessity brought in its train some dangers, some evils. We do not because of them regret the progress, [-xx-] we face resolutely the evils, bent upon cutting them out if possible; if that is not possible then upon minimizing them, then upon obviating all that can be obviated of their results. We do not regret the progress. The state of Washington would not exist if it were not for that progress, if our people had not gone forward, if we had not [-xx-] within us the desire to conquer the contin ent and then so to handle it as to make it the seat of a tremendous industrial power. We have got those {probelms|problems}; we have got to face them. The way to do is not to shirk them, not to regret them; they are here; to accept them as part of the penalty of our growth and greatness, to be ashamed to whine about them, and to do our level best in a spirit of courage and sanity and of broad generous brotherhood to try to solve them.

As the new problems arise we need new methods of solution; but there is {ro|no} change in the spirit in which we are to approach them. In '61 it was one set of problems; in 1903, in 1913, in [-xxx-] 1923 there will be others[+,+] not quite as heavy[+,+] but others[+,+] and we can solve them only by approaching them in the spirit in which the generation that acknowledged Ab raham Lincoln as a chief approached their problems. (Applause) Sever al elements are necessary for [-xxxx-] them; in the first place to approach them in a spirit of loyal effort to do good to our fellow men, of a genuine disinterestedness , the effort that recognizes that each of us is his brother's keeper and must try to help that brother out; but while I want to see us approach them in a spirit as far rem oved as possible from hardness of heart, I al so want to see us approach these problems in a spirit as far removed as possible from softness of head. You can help a man when he slips. Every one of us slips at times; every one of us needs such help; it is a shame to any of us if he does not stretch out his hand to help his brother who slips;
[pb 112 425:0481] if the man lies down you cannot carry him. He has got to walk himself; and if he is willing to let you carry him you {ca n|can} make up your mind that that fact alone shows that he is not worth carrying.

I ask that we take up our life work here at home as individuals bound to see justice, to see that the laws give the fairest opportunity for each man that laws can give, and that they do absolute justice among all men without regard to their wealth, their social station, or their occupation; and furthermore that we approach these new problems with the old problems with the old, rugged [-xx-] American spirit of self-help, self-reliance, of desire each to {shpw|show} that he can carve out his own life without damage to his fellows and yet with success for himself.

Our foreign policy can be summed up very briefly. We must not wrong the weak and we must not flinch from the strong; (Cheers and applause) we must avoid that sure sign of the weak man or the weak nation -- bluster. D o not threaten; do not talk. In the old days when I was in the cow business myself we had a saying : "Never draw unless you mean to shoot." That is a good policy for the nation too. Do not say anything in the way of threat or abuse of other nations; always speak courteously of them. If it becom es absolutely necessary to say anything make it good afterwards. In other words make the nation just exactly the type of manly quality which we prize among ou[-[illegible]-][+r+] fellow men. (Cheers and applause)
