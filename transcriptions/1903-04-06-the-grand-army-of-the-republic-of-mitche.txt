Let me say a word more to you suggested by this gift. I spoke of the qualities shown by the men of the Grand Army in the civil war. One of those qualities was the recognition of each man for his worth as a man. You men who fought in that war remember well enough what it meant; you recollect when at 10 o'clock in the {morningthe|morning the} blanket was too heavy[+,+] and if you were a raw recruit you threw it away,- but at ten that night it became not heavy enough. You remember the fatigue, the endless marches [-in-] under the southern sun and the freezing misery of the nights in winter, the misery of the fever cots in the hospital, and the great battle, and its loss, and its terror, and the death of the bravest and brightest who shed their blood like water around you. When you got into that kind of work you got to paying heed as to what the man was who was on you right hand or your left. It was of [-xxx-] great consequence to you to know it. {Youdid|You did} not care at all what way he worshipped his Maker; you did not care whether he had been born here or elsewhere; you did not care whether he was a banker or bricklayer, whether he was a farmer or a merchant, or a mechanic. What you wanted to know was, whether he would stay put when the time came. You cared to know the qualities that stood for his being a man, and if you had managed the army on any other basis we would have failed as sure as fate. We won when we chose our generals[+/+] because

[pb 69 425:0227]

of their worth as generals. Grant, Sherman, Sheridan and Thomas, and Farragut at sea, were chosen because they were first class fighting men and could do best the work that was to be done. That is how you handled the affairs of the army, and that is why you were able to bring to a successful conclusion the great civil war.

The same thing [-[illegible]-] has to be done in civil life. We cannot {affordto|afford to} draw artificial distinctions in citizenship any more than you could afford to draw them in the army. We never can make and keep this government at the high pitch where we have a right to expect it will be brought and kept until we get it ingrained in us to remember that it is a foul wrong to the country to treat any man[+|+] save [-xx-] on his worth as a man. There are two sides to it. It is a foul wrong to behave with arrogance and lack of sympathy toward those who are less fortunate; and it is equally as foul a wrong to show envy and rancor and hatred toward those who[+a+]re more fortunate than ourselves. The worst lesson that can be taught any American is to teach him to suspect, to feel jealousy of, and hatred[+/+]for, his fellow Americans. I believe so thoroughly that the average American is a pretty good fellow that I feel that what we chiefly need is to have him [-[?brought into contact,?]-] find the viewpoint of any other average American , in order to have them work well together. In the long run our interests are common. In any system dealing with eighty millions of men it must be taken as general. There are exceptions, of course, but in the long run we are going to go up or go down together. When you had hard times here it did not just strike here The hard times went from New York to San Francisco. They did not respect state lines. They went everywhere. Now that good times have come, they go everywhere. When you had hard times and this town was struck, it struck all. Some people went down more than others; and when good times came some went up faster and further than others. That was partly luck and partly due to the individual characteristics of the people. But fundamentally when we go down all of us go somewhat down, and when we go up all {ofus|of us} go more or less up. It is just the same as it was in the [?army?]. When the defeat came, the whole army felt it. You could not smash one wing and have the center and the other wing {pefectly|perfectly} happy, because they were not. It is just the same thing now as a whole. We can win prosperity partly because of [?Providence?] being with us, but chiefly if under Providence we develop sanity, [?common?] sense, courage, {hardinhood|hardihood}, stoutness of heart, and stoutness of body [?among?] our own citizens.

(Cheers and applause)
