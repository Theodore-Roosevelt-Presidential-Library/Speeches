Mr. Senator, Mr. Chairman, Ladies and Gentlemen:

It is a great pleasure to be here with you in this beautiful {prairiex|prairie} state. I wish to thank beyond all the men of the Grand Army[-xxx-] who came out to greet me to-day. Never do I stop in Kansas that I [-xxxx-] fail to see some {rupresentatives|representatives} of {thosd|those} who were true in the days that tried men's souls. I want to thank you for one {reminiscente|reminiscence} of old times, that is the horns of the steer here. I used to be in the cow business myself. But in the days when I went into it 20 years ago the cattle looked more like that than they do now. They were pretty nearly all tails and horns in those days, whereas [-xxxx-] as I go along now and look out on the ranges they are[+a+] first class quality of high-grade stock, and I congratulate you upon them. This place inevitably recalls the old frontier days, the days when the pioneer had to do work as hard, and often almost as dangerous as the man who went into the great army from '61 to '65. Now that has all been changed. We have seen grow up farms, groves, the green of the alfalfa and corn in so many places that only knew the short grass of the prairie. I am out in the western part of the state in the short-grass district, and I am getting more and more among the people whose like I knew so well. I worked on the range myself for a number of years. I lived [-[?for a number of years?]-] in the country farther north than this, but in the real western country, and it makes me feel at home to see you all now. I congratulate you {ypon|upon} what you have done with the prairie; upon the way in which you have turned it into farms and [-[illegible]-] ranches; upon what you have done with it agriculturally; upon what you have done with it pastorally; and more and more as our people have grown accustomed so as to know what can and what cannot be done with the prairie, [-[illegible]-] more and more the chance of success has grown. We have come to understand that there are places where you can raise cattle and cannot raise wheat; we have come to know more and more what it is possible to attempt. My fellow citizens, I congratulate you upon the prosperity which we are all now enjoying. If providence, if {fortunemlays|fortune lays} its hand

[pb 44 425:0347]

heav[-y-][+ily+] upon us then it may be impossible for any skill or forethought or resolution entirely to overcome it. If drought comes, if some {oth er|other} misfortune comes, then it is impossible to avoid a certain amount of suffering and misery, but in the course of the years that evens up and there remains more success to the element of our own personal effort. Something can be done by law. We need wise laws; we need honest and fearless administration of the law; and a bad law or a corrupt and inequitable enforcement of the law must inevitably do damage. But more than the law, more than the enforcement of the law, it rests with the individual himself to {achiefe|achieve} success. No law that ever was devised by the wit of man will make a coward brave, or a fool wise, or a weakling strong. What law can do is to prevent one man's doing injustice to another, and then to give the fairest field possible for the display of the talents that each man may have. I congratulate you on what you have done in taming the prairie. I congratulate you on the churches and the schools. They mean that you are training the higher life of the {cauntry|country}. I congratulate you on all your crops. I want to say that the one that I like best is the crop of children. I believe in you and I like your kind, and want to see it kept up.(Cheers and applause.)
