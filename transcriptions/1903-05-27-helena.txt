Mr. Governor;  Mr. Mayor;  My Fellow Citizens:

It is a great pleasure to have the chance of saying a few words of greeting to you this morning. You are not strangers to me. It is twenty years ago that I first came into Montana;  and the eastern part of your state, the cow country, I knew well. (Cheers and applause) There is one town in the easternmost part of Montana where I came pretty near being the oldest living inhabitant. From the very beginning I have believed implicitly in you and your state;  and no man outside of [-the-][+its+] limits [-of your state-] has rejoiced more sincerely as that belief has been made good by the events. (Applause)

In thanking all of you for your greeting to-day, I know that the rest will not object to my saying a special word of thanks to the men of the great civil war, to the men of the Grand Army, who occupy for all time the right of the line in the march of civic affairs. (Applause) It pleased me so to-day to see together with the men who wore the blue, representatives of the men who wore the gray. [-fxx-] Our war, terrible and awful though it was, has been in its outcome the most beneficent war which history has ever known;  for that war left us not only a reunited country,guaranteed to us at the cost of four years of bloodshed, for ever and aye within our own borders, guaranteed to us an ever growing greatness as one people among the nations of mankind;  not only did it do that, but it left us the right of brotherhood with the gallant vanquished, it left every American the right to feel a keen thrill of pride in every deed of valor, every deed of self-devotion, done in that war alike by those who so gallantly wore the blue and by those who so gallantly wore the gray. (Applause) All Americans are the better to-day because of the self-devotion, the courage, the steadfastness, the endurance, the iron resolution, shown by the men of the civil war, by the men who followed Grant, and by those with whom you of the Grand Army by your victory gave us the right of countrymen-- by those who followed Lee.

I wish furthermore to say a word of special greeting to my own comrades, the men of the Spanish-American war;  and to the men of the big war (for ours was a little one) I do not have to say that there is no tie [+that binds a+] man quite as close to any other man as the tie of having served in the same war. Of course [-ix-] [-xxxx-] we had a difficulty from which you of [+'+]61 to '65 were entirely free-- in our case there [pb 121 425:0486] was not enough war to go around. (Laughter and applause) You did not have any trouble of that kind. It was characteristic of Montana, and to be expected, that her sons should leap eagerly forward in '98 when the flag again called for service. You could not help it;  There were too many old soldiers in this state ;  and the pioneers, the men who made this state, fundamentally showed exactly the same qualities that the men of the great civil war showed, the same qualities of hardihood, of resolution, of daring, of willingness to take risks for a sufficient object-- with a state founded by, and made up, of such people, naturally your sons leaped forward when the chance came for them in their turn to show themselves the equals of their sires.

It is a great pleasure to come through this state, and to see legibly written for the most unobservant to read the assured promise of the future greatness. I sometimes think that you, yourselves, do not altogether realize how great that future will be. Your mines count for much;  your ranches count for much;  but most of all is going to be done by the water; (Applause) and in two ways. In the first place, thanks to the rapid fall of the rivers from the mountains, there is a well-nigh inexhaustible source of power in your streams, which will certainly be used for the building up of great [?manufactories?]. We are going to see great {manuafcturing|manufacturing} centers here in Montana taking advantage of the power of your waters. In the next place these waters will be used under wise schemes of irrigation until you make this whole state blossom like a rose. You need, first of all, to distribute the water in space through the irrigating ditches, and then to preserve it in time by storage reservoirs; so as to keep the floods that run to waste at one season for use at the season when they are most needed. The Congress, the national legislature, has not of recent years put upon the statute book any law as wise and as beneficent as the national irrigation act of a year ago. (Applause) It marks the beginning of the scheme of using aright the waters which have been allowed to go to waste;  and as all of you know, when irrigation becomes an accomplished fact and the waters are used in accordance with the right principles of irrigation we always find that rainfall is a very poor substitute for it. We are past in irrigation the stage of preliminary experiments. There is no question [-xx-] of what can be done by it;  the question merely is as to the methods, as to the means, of making it most effective. In my [?judgment?] the greatest development [pb 122 425:0486] within our borders, the greatest development not on the seacoast of the United States, during the next half century will be the development of what have been called the arid and semi-arid lands, under the application of the principles of irrigation. I want to say one thing with emphasis. In endeavoring to secure the adoption of the irrigation law I was met with protest from people dwelling in the humid regions, who believed that the building up of agriculture in an [-in-][+ext+]tensive form on the arid regions through irrigation would be detrimental to them. It cannot too often be said that it is never detrimental to [-xxxxx-]any section of this country to have another section built up. In the long run we are going to up or go down together. In the long run what benefits one part [-xf-] is going to benefit more or less all parts. The old thirteen colonies, especially in the north east, a century ago looked with great anxiety and nervousness upon the settlement and growth of the Ohio valley and Great Lakes region. They thought that it would [-be damaging-][+mean damage+] to them to have new and great [-xxxxxx-] agricultural states grow up between the Alleghenies and the Mississippi and the Great Lakes. It did not mean damage to them. They have grown too. Both sections have grown;  and the country of course profited immensely . So in the same way it will be an advantage to all of the states in the Mississippi valley and eastward of the Alleghenies when these states of the Rocky Mountains and the great plains grow in wealth, in resources, and in population. With every growth of irrigated agriculture in this country there will come a corresponding growth of manufacturing at once, a growth in commerce, a growth in population, which population will itself largely absorb the products of that irrigated agriculture, and which will demand further products from the eastern centers of manufacture and population. The growth of the Rocky Mountain states [-xxxxx-] in greatness means further growth to the communities east and west of them. It does not mean detriment to the other states of the Union;  it means advantage to them;  it means a help {tothe m|to them};  just exactly as prosperity in the eastern states means prosperity in the {Rowky|Rocky} Mountains. Prosperity does not stop at state lines;  it goes right along over them. I am sorry to say adversity does the same thing. Our people need to have it made part of their fiber, need to have it put into their inmost thought and habits of feeling, that [-xx-][+any+] development in any section of the country is a good thing for all the country;  so I [-xxxxxxxxxxx-] not only congratulate you upon the growth to [pb 123 425:0487] greatness of your state, but I congratulate the entire Union upon that growth. (Applause)

In the irrigation work a beginning has been made. We will have to feel our way along, because it is important not to make mistakes. The nation can do a good deal;  the state governments themselves can do a good deal;  most of all of course must be done by the individual citizens themselves, either individually or in associations;  but one policy we must have clearly settled in our own minds as the one to follow -- the whole aim of what we do in irrigation must be to put actual home makers on the land. We must not permit the scheme of irrigation to be diverted from its proper purpose;  to be diverted into allowing large tracts of land to be held by single individuals or by companies for speculative objects. That is not the proper purpose. (Cheers and applause) Our object, which we must pursue unswervingly, must be to divide the irrigated lands into tracts of suitable size each for the actual home maker who dwells on it and cultivates it, who intends to live in the country and to bring up his children to be citizens of it. That is the type of man for whose welfare we must work;  because he is the man who makes ultimately the best type of citizen for the state itself.

Something, and I think a good deal, can be done both by the nation and the state in irrigation. It was essential that both should act;  but after all the only thing that the government can do is to give the chance to the average man to work out his own salvation under reasonably favorable circumstances. No act, no law, no scheme of law, can ever supply the place of the man's own individual qualities. It is just as it was in war, as the veterans know;  you could give a man a nice gun, a nice uniform, even train him, and if he did not have the right stuff in him you could not get it out of him. (Applause) Weapons are very necessary. I think all of us who have had the experience do not want black powder muskets when the other side has smokeless powder {nuskets|muskets}. If two men are pretty evenly matched the man with the best weapon will win. But if a man has not got the fighting edge in him you can give him any weapon [-[?alike?]-] and he will be beaten by a good man with a club. Now that is true on the other side. It is just the same in citizenship. Exactly as in battle it is the man behind the gun who counts most (the gun counts too, don't think that I fail to understand that) so in any community it is the man behind the law, the man who acts under and through the law who really counts in the long run in building up a nation. There are [pb 124 425:0487] other nations with constitutions modeled exactly on ours , with laws like ours, where nevertheless the net out[-xxx-] come of national growth has been as different [-xx-] from ours as one pole is from the other;  because the type of citizenship was different. We have progressed as a nation partly because of the natural advantages, partly because of our {consititution|constitution}, our laws, our system of government, but chiefly because of the average quality of the average man and woman who went to make up our people;  (Applause) and so long as we can keep up the [-xxxxxxxx-] average standard of our citizenship, just so long we can count upon going steadily forward in our allotted work. To do that we need more than one quality. We need stout bodies;  and we need even more strong minds;  and finally we need what counts for more than body and more than mind -- character;  character,which is the all-essential element in the growth of a nation {andin|and in} the growth of an individual;  character, into which many elements enter, but three above all;  the quality of honesty, for without that, ability physical or mental [-xxxxx-] merely makes a man a curse to his neighbors and to the state;  the quality of honesty first , and in addition the quality of courage, of hardihood, the quality to which we allude when we speak of a man as not merely a good man, but emphatically a man;  and in addition thereto, in addition to honesty, in addition to courage, the saving grace of common sense. I believe in your future;  I believe in the future of this nation;  because I believe that the average American citizen has in him just those qualities of honesty, of courage, and of common sense. (Cheers and applause)
