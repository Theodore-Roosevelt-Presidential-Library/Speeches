My fellow-citizens:

I thank you all for your greeting . Let me say first a word of acknowledgment to the Grand Arm y, through the grand daughter of one of whose members I was presented with these flowers. It has been a delight to me this morning to get into the state of Washington. It is a state already great but with possibilities of growth such as I believe its own citizens hardly realize; for the state of Washington within the lifetime of the younger among those whom I am addressing, will rank as among the [-x-] most populous and most wealthy [-x-] in the entire union. Although I am glad to see already evidence of your material well-being, material prosperity, what I most congratulate you upon is the way in which you are providing for the growth without which material prosperity counts for so little -[-[illegible]-]the growth of the higher life. Wherever I have been I have noticed the attention paid to your schools, paid to the education of the young; and that such a course is wise hardly needs mention. (Applaus e/) The greatest material resources will amount to nothing if you have not the right type of men to take advantage of them; just exactly as in the civil war, it made no difference as to the arms or the training you gave a man, if he did not have the right stuff in him you could not get it out of him. (Applause.) Our [-[?soldiers?]-] men became such matchless soldiers because they had in them the quality out of which heroes are made; so in our citizenship, great though our physical advantages are in the nation as a whole, peculiarly great though they are in this state, they would amount to nothing if you did not have the right kind of men and women to take advantage of them. I believe in your future, and am convinced of the future of the republic because I am confident that the average {ci izen|citizen} of the republic has the right stuff in him, (Applause.) to make a soldier in war, to make a good citizen in time of peace; and in peace and in war the first consideration is doing the duty that comes to hand, and do ing it, not shirking it, not asking whether it is easy and pleasant, but doing it. You know that in the army the man that counted was [+the man+] that did whatever there was to do , and if he did not do it you did not want him as a neighbor in the {amy|army}. So in civil life it is [pb 64 425:0457] the constant performance of the duty that comes up day by day that makes the good citizen, that makes the man upon whom one can depend for help as a neighbor, for duty performed to the state and the nation. I am glad to have seen you; I am glad to have seen the men and women, and I am particularly glad to have seen the children. One thing that has impressed me particularly coming from the Atlantic to the Pacific is the essential unity of our people; the unity for which you fought, and your fellows died in the civil war. We can never afford in this country to permit any man to divide us section against section, creed against creed or class against class: We shall be false to the teachings and the deeds of our fathers if we do not act on the fundamental truth underlying our whole system of government and our national life, the truth [-x-] that each man is entitled to be treated on his worth as a man without regard to aught else. Any division between man and man on the ground of wealth, occupation or locality, is false and un-American. ({Wheers|Cheers} and applause.)
