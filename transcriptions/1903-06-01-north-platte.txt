Mr. Chairman, and you, my fellow-Americans:

It is a pleasure to have the opportunity of greeting you this afternoon; and in greeting all of you, I know that the others will not [-xxxxxx-] object to my saying a special word of thanks for the attendance of the men of the Grand Army,(Applause.) the men to whose deeds we owe it that there is now a common country, and that the President of the United States can travel from the Atlantic to the Pacific, from the Gulf to the Canadian border and find himself everywhere at home. (Cheers and applause.) You must also let me say a word of special greeting to the gentlemen from the range.(Applause.) Day before yesterday, boys, I had a little ride myself, and I wish you had been along. Finally let me greet especially the {fu ure|future}, in the shape of the children. As you gentlemen know, I believe in children, and I am glad to see that apparently the children here are all right in quality and in quantity.

In traveling through our country during the past [-xxxx-][+two+] months, the thing that has impressed me more than aught else is the essential unity of our people. I wish to say to the veterans,the men of the Grand Army, that more than ever, coming back now, I have a keen and lively appreciation of what they did by their deeds in the Civil War, for they warred not only for unity as regards sections, but for unity all through, for brotherhood all through.

The lesson that you men of the civil war taught applies not merely to military life; it applies just as much to civil life. The civil war was won[+,+] when Abraham Lincoln called for men who were willing to die for an ideal[+[?;?]+] it was won in the first place, because we had people who appreciated the real value of things, who appreciated [-[?that often?]-] that often the best things are those that are intangible, that can hardly be touched, that can hardly be called material[+,+] and because as a people we were able to show [-xxxx-] that with us the sense of brotherhood, the sense of treating each man on his merit as a man[+,+] was a real and not a merely nominal part of our character. In the first place, our people in '61 showed
[pb 2 425:0524] a realization of what was the important and what the unimportant side of life, just as I ask you, my countrymen, now to show that realization. I do not preach to an individual nor to this nation the doctrine of the easy life. I do not preach to any one to try to be happy by avoiding difficulties, avoiding all trouble, all worry, all work. The kind of life led by the man whose one aim is to have the easiest time possible[+,+] to my mind is not worth living. In 186[-x-] [+[-xxx-]+] when Lincoln [+,+] called to arms the easy thing to do was not to answer , and the easy lives were led by those who did not respond to the call. Now understand me, I know perfectly well that there were many men who for one reason or another could not and properly did not respond to the call; but there were plenty of men, who when the call was made found it pleasanter and easier to stay at home and wonder why you did not take Richmond that summer than to go ahead and try to take it. It was a very different job talking about it and doing it, because there were a lot of obstinate men in gray in the way[+—+] fellow-Americans, with whom your prowess and your victory has now given [-x-] us the right of brotherhood. One of the pleasant things, one of the wonderful things of that war was that the victory gave to the victors the right again of resuming brotherhood with the vanquished. That was one of the wonderful feats of that war. The man who stayed at home slept at ease under a roof; he did not have to work, he did not have to face risk. You who went out, went out to lie night after night through the winter weather in the frozen mud of the trenches, to march in the scorching heat of the southern sun, when as you remember, if {your|you} were recruits, about noon the blanket got so heavy that you tended to throw it away, and at midnight you wished [-xx-] you had two; you went to suffer cold and heat, thirst and wearing fatigue, to face death and see the brightest and the bravest pour out their blood like water on the stricken field, or give up their lives on the fever cots of the hospitals; you did all that. You did it and you did it gladly, because you knew what counted in life and what did not. You knew that there were things infinitely more important than the life of ease, the life of mere pleasure,
[pb 3 425:0525] the life that desired above all things that it should go softly in the delicate places of the earth. Now in looking back, which is the life worth having lived? The life of the man that stayed at home or the life of the man who went out? You who went out have [-x-] handed on not merely to your children and your children[+'+]s children, but to all of us who come after you a {hemitage|heritage} of honor forever more. You have made the whole nation your debtor for al[+l+] time; and as long as history speaks of the American republic it will speak of the deeds of the men who saved it.(Applause.) Wasn't that worth doing?

I ask that in civil life, in social and industrial life we apply practically the same principles that these men applied who marched to war in the blue uniform from '61 to '65 . The kind of life that is worth living is not the easy life, is not the life passed in avoidance of trouble and worry, in shirking duties, in endeavoring only to have a pleasant time. If there exists any set of people more miserable than others in our country it is those at either end of the social scale whose aim in life it is only to have a pleasant time. I do not know of any man more sure to be unhappy in the long run than the man who sets himself down [-x-] for his life work to try to have a pleasant time only. The only life worth living is the life that has as its goal duty performed, that has as its goal the doing of a work that is worth doing and doing it well. It is just exactly as in bringing up your children. We all of us know unwise fathers, I am sorry to say unwise mothers too, who will tell you, well I have had to work hard and my boy shall not,[-xxxx-] and if it is a mother she will say, I have had to work hard, my girl[-x-] shall be [-xxxxxxxx-] [+a+] lady, meaning thereby that the girl shall be a curse to herself and every body else by not being trained to work. They think they are being affectionate to the children. They are not, they are foolish. Of course every man[-x-] , every woman, who i s a father, who is a mother, will try to take care to give their children happiness , to educate them well, to see that things are made as pleasant for them as is consistent with teaching them how to do their duty. It is not a kindness, it
[pb 4 425:0525] is a curse to bring the child up so that it [-shall-][+will+] not be able to do its work in the world afterwards. It is a curse to a boy to bring him up at home under conditions which make him wholly unable to hold his own when he gets out, as he must get out in the end, to try to do his work in real life. I ask you to bring up your children not to shirk obstacles, but to [-xxxxx-][+face+] them and overcome them. I ask you to have them carry on their lives as you carry on yours[-xxxxxxxx-] with the object not of finding out how {troublecan|trouble can} be avoided, but how the best life can be lived. That is sound doctrine in morals and in practice. As I ask you to do that in the sphere of the individual citizen I ask that the nation follow out the same course. A little nation can without [-[illegible]-][+shame+] play a small part. A big nation must play a big part. It is not open to us as a people to say whether or not we are to play a big part in the world. We cannot help it. We have got to play a big part. We can play it badly if we wish, but play it well or play it badly we have to. We shall either succeed greatly or fail greatly. It is only open to us to choose which of the two it shall be. I know you, my fellow countrymen, too well to hesitate as to what your choice will be. The nation should shape its conduct as the individual citizen shapes his. We cannot succeed as a people unless we have the same standard of honesty, decency, in public life as in private life, and the same standard of honor among our public men.

We have great problems here within our own borders. We must try to face them and solve them. Something can be done by law, a good deal at times; more can be done by honest {andequal|and equal} enforcement of the law, of law that recognizes no distinction between the rich man or poor man; not in favor of one, or in favor of the other, but favoring the decent man, the law-abiding man, be he rich or be he poor. (Applause) We must have the type of character in public life that we demand of a man with whom we deal in private life. My friends on horseback, in the old days I lived in the cow country myself, and then we had an expression that ran: "Don't draw unless you mean to shoot." I have great contempt for a private individual who is always blustering, boasting and {threatenting|threatening} and when the times comes does not make good.

I want our foreign policy as a nation carried on along just exactly those lines[+.+] avoid speaking ill of or insulting any friendly power; always deal courteously with others; be scrupulously careful not to wrong the weak, and hold ourselves ready so as to see that we are not wronged by the strong; (Cheers and
[pb 5 425:0526] applause) do not take a position until we are sure we mean to keep it, and when we have decided to keep it make good whatever we say. (Cheers and applause) I think that is a fairly sound rule for a good foreign policy[+. ¶+] I want you people of Nebraska, and I want the people of every state in this Union, to help me in building up and keeping built up the arm of the government that is essential,-- the navy of the United States. (Applause) Nothing has pleased me more than the way in which the response to the appeal for the navy has been as instantaneous in the interior of this nation as on the seacoast [-[?wither?]-][+whether+] of the Atlantic or the Pacific ocean[+,+] and it is right it should be so. Exactly as the great battleships are named after all the states, so every deed of honor they perform reflects credit upon each and all of us, and humiliation of the navy of the United States would mean humiliation to every American citizen worth calling such. A navy cannot be built up when war has begun. Those vast and delicate and formi[+d-+]able mechanisms[-,-]-- modern warships -- must be prepared well in advance, and then the men trained how to use them. In battle the only shots that count are the shots that hit. There is no earthly use in putting the finest type of gun aboard a ship if you have not got the gunner that can point it right. We need then the ships, we need the guns; we have got in America[-[?n?]-] the right type of m[-a-][+e+]n, and should have the right type of m[-a-][+e+]n trained so that [+[?t?]+]he[+[?y?]+] can shoot straight[-,-] and handle themselves right. If we do that we will have in the navy, in the first place, the surest guaranty of peace. [-I think that foreign powers [?generally mean?] well toward us, but I think it helps them [?out?] if we have a good navy. It will encourage them to persevere in their good intentions.-] It is the surest guaranty of peace, the greatest insurance against war, and also an assurance that if war comes the flag will come out of it with its glory undimmed.

My friends, it has been the greatest pleasure for me to see you. I believe in you with all my heart. I wish you well , and I know that the future of this country is assured because I know what type of man the average American citizen is. (Cheers and applause)
