Mr. {Govermor|Governor}; Mr. Mayor; and you, My Fellow Citizens; My Fellow Americans, Men and Women of Washington:

I am glad to have the chance of coming to your Capital, to welcome the people of this state, and to thank them for greeting me. Let me say a word first to those whom you have placed first, to the men of the Grand Army,(Applause) to the men who coming from many states fought in one uniform and under one flag for the country that belongs to us all, to the men to whom we owe it that now a man can travel throughout the length and breadth of this land and be at home everywhere because he is among his fellow Americans everywhere. Then, furthermore, a word of special greeting to those among the early pioneers who happen to be present, (Applause) to the men who showed fundamentally the same qualities that were shown by you of the great civil war; because they too had to go out to dare, to do, to accomplish feats of difficulty and daring, feats that needed iron hardihood and iron resolution, because they too contemptuously cast aside the life of mere ease, the life of effortless pleasure, to take hold of the nobler life of difficulty rewarded by triumph in the end. (Applause) I have come here to the capital of a giant commonwealth, a commonwealth still in its youth growing by leaps and bounds and with a future even greater than its [-xxxx-] people themselves now believe; for I doubt if any of us entirely realize what the future of Washington is bound to be, because of the wonderful resources which you have within your state limits; and proud indeed is the privilege of belonging to a {genemation|generation} and living in a locality where what has been done here in this state is being done at the present moment. Coming through your state to-day and in Oregon yesterday as I have met the older men who have seen this progress take place under their eyes, I have felt that in all the ages the time best worth living in has been the last half century and the place best living in the American republic (Applause); because it has been a half century of work,[-xxx-][+of+] effort, of strife, strife with men, {strige|strife} with nature. I pity no man because he has had to strive, because he has had to work hard; on the contrary I envy him the privilege of having been able to show himself a man doing a man's work. (Applause) The greatest of all things in life is the chance of having a great work to do and then doing it. (Applause) Here in our country there should be but little {moom|room} for any[-[?xxx?]-] [pb 66 425:0458] who teach, still less for those [-xxxx-] who would practice, the life of mere ease, of mere enjoyment. Every now and then one sees a father, I am sorry to say sometimes a mother, who bring up their children not to take up the {tasksthat|tasks that} come and do them, but with that most foolish of ideas , the idea of sparing them from all necessity for effort. I think each one of you will remember among his or her friends where that is true; where the father says: "I have worked hard, my boys shall enjoy themselves." You have also seen where the mother slaves so that the dau ghters can be idle. That is not showing affection for the child; that is showing folly. That is not affection; it is foolishness; just as foolish if the man and woman are very rich as if the man and woman are very poor. The parent who really loves the child will train the child not to shirk difficulties but to meet and overcome them. (Applause) If the boy or girl is turned out from a home in which everything has been made soft and no faithful performance of irksome or difficult duty taught [-xxxx-], that child is turned out with the certainty that it will be a curse to itself and to all around it, and unable to do its part in our American life.

You men of '61 to '65, there were others at that time who during those four years led lives that were easier than the lives you led; there were others that stayed at home and walked delicately in the earth's soft places; that slept warm and sheltered when you slept under ragged blankets on the frozen mud in the trenches; they did little,except criticise you, while you marched under the southern sun; when you fought and lost or won[+,+] but if you lost came back to fight again[+;+] they stayed at ease well fed, well clothed, sheltered. [-xxxx-] Which is the life worth having lived, theirs or yours, and are you to be pitied because you toiled, because you knew sweat and blood, because you knew hunger and the grinding fatigue, because you faced risk, because you saw those around the brightest and the best die on the battlefield[+,+] [?or?] on the fever cots of the hospital? No. You are to be envied, but not pitied. [-It-]

It is so in life. It is given to but few generations to have the chance that you men had in your generation. Honor, thrice honor, to those who take advantage of the chance when it comes. We at this time have not the opportunities to do wh at the men of the great {civilwar|civil war} did. We have not the opportunity to do the hard, the rough, the infinitely valuable of the early pioneers, but we have our own problems and we must approach them in the spirit shown by the [pb 67 425:0459] pioneers, in the spirit shown by the men of the civil war, if we are to do our duty in our turn and make this nation what it shall be made, what it will be made;-a nation great in its past but in its future not only great but the greatest among the nations of all time. (Cheers and applause)

I would preach to no man the doctrine of the easy life. I would {preachto|preach to} every man the life of work for a worthy end, the life of decency, of square dealing, of honest behavior in the family, among one[+'+]s neighbors, to the state; and so I would preach to the nation not the course that is easiest, but the course that is the greatest. (Applause) In '61 the easy thing to do was to say that the union was dissolved and could not be put together again; that was the easy thing to say and many people said it, but you did the other thing. You can all remember the days immediately after '61 when any number of excellent people said that the battle of Bull Run showed that the war had come to an end; but it hadn't; it lasted three years and a half longer and then it ended the other way. Our nation rose and we walk with our heads high be cause we are Americans to-day, be cause of the spirit that drove you and your fellows onward thro ugh trial to triumph in '65. We in our turn must have in us that spirit. We have plenty of problems, plenty of difficulties to face at home and abroad; we have problems within our own borders and problems outside. Each generation has its difficulties . In e ach generation those difficulties , those {probelems|problems}, take new shape, but the spirit necessary to overcome the [-xxxx-] difficulties, the spirit necessary for the solution of the problems, does not change from generation to generation. In '61 the men who wore the blue had different weapons and different [-xx xx-] tactics from the Continental soldi ery in blue and buff who followed Washington from Valley {Fprge|Forge} to Yorktown, and now your sons, the men whom this state and the other states sent over to the Philippines there to win fresh honor for the stars and stripes, those men have other weapons than those with which you fought; they {haveto|have to} fight in open order without the elbow {tough|touch} of the old tactics, but the spirit that drives them forward is the spirit of Gettysburg and Appomattox. The war of '98 was only a skirmish. In it we hope that we showed that we had the spirit to try to do as big a job as yours if it had been necessary.

In our new complex industrial civilization great problems have [?arisen.?] [?With?] all the benefits that have accrued there has come a certain amount [?of evil.?] [pb 68 425:0459] we are not to be excused if we fail to do all that in us lies to cut out or at least to minimize that evil. New devices must be imagined and applied to work out the solution for the new problems; but we must approach them in the same old spirit, the spirit of the colleagues of Washington who founded the government , of the followers of Abraham Lincoln who preserved it; (Applause) a spirit compounded of many elements but especially of three or four. In the first place, the spirit of decency, of honesty, of fair dealing between man and man. If we have not that then it is idle to hope for success for the [-xxxxx-], state, for the nation. There must be the foundation of morality and decency. One of the least admirable qualities of any people is the quality sometimes shown by men whom we know quite well who tend to deify mere smartness, as they call it, unaccompanied by any sense of moral responsibility. Too often you will hear a man say of some scoundrel: "Well, he is smart". You say "Yes, he is a crook" and the[-y-] answer, [+is+] "Well, may be he is, but he is dre'ful smart"; and it is meant as praise. That kind of praise is a disgrace to the man giving it and to the man receiving it. In the civil war the braver and stronger a man was, if he was a traitor his courage and strength only made him more dangerous; if he did not have in him the root of loyalty to the flag, to the country, to his companions all his ability simply rendered him more able to do harm. It is just the same way in civil life. The acuteness of mind like great bodily [-xxxxxx-] strength merely serves to ren der the possessor a greater menace to the state if it is not guided by principle, morality, decency. The able man who preys on his fellows is a curse to the entire community. More and more we must strive to bring about the day when the successful rascal will be [-xxxxxx-][+hounded+] down as the unsuccessful rascal is . (Applause) The man who [-xxxxx-] for a personal motive seeks to get his fellows to adopt a course dangerous to the commonwealth is an enemy to all, including especially those to whom he makes his appeal. The appeal can be made in two ways -- the spirit of arrogant disregard of the rights of those who are less well off; the spirit that seeks to exploit for its own benefit the weakness of others; the spirit that makes a man of wealth look down on or disregard or scheme against the man less well off or less well able to hold his own in the struggle for material things, that is one of the basest and most evil of spirits. But there is one equally base, that is the spirit which teaches envy, hatred, and rancor toward men because they are well off, because they have the goods of this world. The appeal to the spirit of [pb 69 425:0460] arrogant disregard of the rights of those less well off or the appeal to the spirit of envy and hatred of those better off, either appeal is equally base and equally un-American. (Applause) This government is not and never shall be the government either of a plutocracy or of a mob. It shall be the government which you fought to make it, you men of '61, a government of liberty by and through the law. (Applause)

You have got to have then, first, decency, mor[-[illegible]-]ality, the spirit of fair and square dealing, the spirit that makes a man a good husband, a good father, [+(+]and if he is not a decent man in his family he will be a {mightypoor|mighty poor} man outside his family in the long run[+)+] the spirit that makes him a good neighbor, a square man to deal with in public {cacapcity|capacity}-- you have got to have that first and you have got to have a good deal more than that. It is only the foundation. In the civil war it did not make any difference how patriotic a man was if he ran away. You have got to have in addition courage, hardihood, strength, the will to dare, to do, to resolve[?,?] to win triumph, to be able to show yourself a man capable of working among his fellow men. In civil life the thoroughly nice good man who [-xxxx-] sits at home in his own parlor and says how bad the world is never did any good to anyone yet, and the perfectly nice man who does not cheat any other [-xx xxx one-][+and+] is perfectly [-xxxxxxx-][+helpless+] himself, when brought into business he is [+of+] very scant use also. I want the har mlessness of the dove, but I want [-[?it?]-] combined with the wisdom of the serpent.

In addition to decency, I wish to see courage, hardihood, manliness, and finally I wish to see in addition to both decency and courage, sanity and commonsense, the power to look {thingscoolly|things coolly} over and not to be led away by any temporary outburst of-[-[illegible]-] sentimentality. I do not like hardness of heart but [-xxxxxxx-][+neither+] do I like softness of head.

All I can {sayin|say in} closing is this. I believe in you; I am proud of you; I believe in you men and women of this state as I believe {ih|in} the men and women of this nation, because I believe that the average man or woman in this state, in this nation, has the three great primary traits; the trait of honesty, the trait of courage, and the saving grace of common sense. (Cheers and applause)
