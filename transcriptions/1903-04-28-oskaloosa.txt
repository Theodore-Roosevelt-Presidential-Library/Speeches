Ladies and Gentlemen:

It is with peculiar pleasure that I come here to take part in the dedication of this Y. M C. A. building. I feel that there is something peculiarly appropriate in the presence of the men who fought in the great war at any ceremony which tends to make for decency, for high thinking, for good citizenship.

I wish first to say one word about your Congressman, Mr. Lacey, at whose request I stop here. In public life generally we are not apt to find the man whose interests go for the [+whole country+] as well as for those who have his immediate fate in their hands; and I [-xxxx-] wish to co ngratulate this district on having in the American Congress a man who {spendshis|spends his} best welfare for the whole United States. (Applause) Gentlemen, I never say before a man what I would not say behind him, or vice versa, and I do not speak hyperbolically, I say what I mean, and I wish to pay this tribute to [-[?Major?]-][+Mr.+] Lacey-- whenever there is a matter that I feel is of real and serious consequence to the nation as a whole, I can ask Mr. Lacey to come to me, or [+can+] go to him, with the absolute certainty that he will approach [-[illegible]-][+the matter+] simply from the standpoint of the public service. He wishes to do well his duty by the public, and the fact that the work is worth doing is a sufficient reward for doing it, (Applause) [-a-][+A+]nd that I regard as high praise for any man of public life.

Now a word about the building itself, what purpose it subserves -- we cannot afford to have our [-xxxxxx-] civilization go on without united and ordered effort on the part of decent people[+,+] to see that the forces of decency have the upper hand. We do not need to bother about the weeds, they will grow anyhow; but the grain needs some careful tending; and nothing augurs better for the future of this country than the way in which efforts are made such as this, which has resulted in the [-xxx-] erection of this association building here. Nature abhors a vacuum, and if you leave a young man's time when he is at liberty absolutely vacant, he will fill it; and[+,+] it is [-[illegible]-] liable, [-[illegible]-] with what is not best worth having in it. Give him occupation, give him the chance to improve himself, make the path fairly easy that leads to clean living and decent work, and you will help him up more than you will by a hundred mere preachings[+;+] you will give him the chance to be decent.

[pb 160 425:0305]

More and more the Young Men's Christian Association [-have-][+has+] tended to do good throughout the nation because [-they have-][+it has+] proceeded in so sane a spirit, a spirit which seeks not to dwarf or suppress healthy instincts, but to get them to turn [-xx-]the right direction, because like all true educational institutions [-they-][+it+] recognise what an education must mean (We are all of us being educated all the time)[+;+] [-[illegible]-] that it can help the body, that it can help what is [+of+] more good than the body --the mind; and finally that it can help what counts for far more than mind and body-- character; the sum of [-[illegible]-] qualities that we call character. That is what counts in the long run. It is a good thing to be a strong man; it is a better thing to be an intelligent and intellectual man; but best of all it is to be a man, a good man, a brave and a strong man. (Applause)

That is what counted, oh my comrades, with you in the great war. When you went into that struggle you went into a struggle that could have been brought to a successful conclusion only by men who to stout bodies and cool heads united the brave heart. That is what counted in the long months of inaction, in the weariness of the marches, in the sleepless vigils of the cold winter nights, and finally in the red-hot fury and agony of the fight. That was when you proved the stuff that there was in the man; then you could see the qualities that you had to have in order to make the man able to do a little more than his fair share. And it is just so in our life now, in our life relative to the state, the life of the man in his own family, or in dealing with his neighbors-- the [-xxx-] thing that counts is the combination of qualities which we call character, at the root of which must lie decency, honesty, the spirit that makes a man treat his fellows squarely and fairly, that makes him a good husband, a good father, a good neighbor, a good man to do business with, a good man to have his property next to yours or to work beside [-[illegible]-] in the shop or on the farm; and decency is not enough. In addition to that you have to have something else; just as you, the men of the great war, needed patriotism, but you had to have something more to make your patriotism stick. You had to have the quality of courage, the quality of hardihood, the quality of iron strength. I do not care how patriotic a man was, if he had a tendency to run away there was nothing to be done with him. So it is now; you have got to have decency, honesty, virtue, morality as the bedrock, but if you have got nothing built upon it it is a poor structure.

[pb 161 425:0306]

The virtue that sits at home and complains that vice has the upper hand, the parlor virtue, the farsighted virtue, that {dees|does} not count. What you need is the good man who is not afraid; the virtue that will go out into the world and try to do something; the decent man who is not afraid of the hurlyburly of actual life,[+—+] and it is rough work, too. Most things that are worth having come by effort. That is true in civil life as in military. You need decency, you need courage, and in addition to both you need the saving grace of common sense. Common sense -- you have got to have that to guide the others aright. {Iti|It} is a mighty good thing to have softness of heart, but it is a pity when the softness extends its area and you get softness of head as well.

I congratulate you, the people of this beautiful city in this beautiful state, upon what you are doing [-xx-] upon the higher life of which the [-xx-] erection of this building is but a symbol. In closing let me say how glad I am to be here, how I have enjoyed coming through Iowa to-day. Let me thank especially the men of the Grand Army for coming out, and then my own comrades, the men of the Nati onal Guard, for coming here to act as my escort. I was pleased to pass so many children. I congratulate Iowa on many things; on her soil, her climate, her crops, but above all on her citizenship. I congratulate her upon the men and women who have made her what she is; (Applause) because, men and women, I do not have to do much preaching to you; I feel rather more like sitting at the feet of Gamaliel; I feel [-like-][+that+] you practice what I want to preach, and I congratulate you on it. Finally, as I said, I like your crops, but as I like your stock the [-xxxxx-] crop I like best to see are the children. I congratulate you on their quality; that is all right, and it seems to me the quantity is all right[+.+] I like your stock and I should be very sorry to see it die out. (Great applause)
