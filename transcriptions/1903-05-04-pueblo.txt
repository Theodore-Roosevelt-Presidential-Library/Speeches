Mr. Mayor[?;?] and you, My Fellow Citizens, Men and Women of Colorado:

I thank you all for your greeting to-day. Most of all I thank the men of the Grand Army for having turned out to greet me. If any of their wives are present I wish to thank them, for the part of the women who waited at home was to the full as hard as that of the men who went to the war, and no soldier did a greater work than the woman who sent husband or son, lover or brother, and who often had to feel not only the agony of wonder as to the fate of the loved one who had gone, but the physical strain caused by the absence of the breadwinner. Let us not forget that. (Applause) I thank you, and I thank you in the name of my own immediate comrades of the lesser war as much as in my own. You were the men who, when the country called to arms, in '98, sprang forward hoping to show that you and I and those like us were not wholly unworthy of the men of the great days. (Applause) You did your duty in the Philippines, you did your duty in keeping unstained the honor of the [-fxx-] flag which you men of the great war had made so precious to us. {Theh|Then} you copied again the example of the men of '61 to '65, for {whem|when} your work was done , back you came to the ranch,the countinghouse, the factory, the mine, and took up your work as decent citizens in private life again. That is why I think we have a right to claim for the American soldier, be he regular or only enlisted for a given war as was the case with most of us and most of you, a certain meed of praise, for he comes forward when the country needs him and when the need is over he goes back to civil life to serve his country again in another way. (Applause)

My fellow citizens, men and women of Colorado, the men of the great war taught us by their lives another lesson besides what they taught us in battle and campaign-- they taught us the lesson which we need to apply now in time of peace, at this stage of our industrial development at the outset of the twent[?i?]eth century, the essential lesson of brotherhood and of treating each man on his merits as a man. You in the big war, and you and I in {thelesser|the lesser} war, soon learned that each man has got to stand on his own feet, and {tha t|that} [?yet?] he has got to work together with his fellows. Every man needs to be helped, and each ought to assist him when the chance comes. You know perfectly well that

[pb 63 425:0357]

[?there were?] some people {i n|in} your own regiment that you could not [?help;?] [?the?] man that always turned up with an excuse for not having been at the [?right place?] at the right time. We have all seen an occasional type of man like that. You can do nothing with that type of man. No organization, no training, nothing else will help him. You can help him temporarily. You have got to have the right quality of man then you have got to get him to work in combination with his fellows. You have got to have the quality that enables him to [+do+] good work by himself and then he has got to join with his fellows in doing good work. We cannot stand alone. We must work together; but if any man chooses to lie down, you cannot carry him. He is not worth carrying. That is one lesson.

A second lesson was the treating of each man on his worth as a man. I do not suppose that there is any place where one learns [-quicker-][+more quickly+] to test a man for the quality there is [-x-] in him than in the campaign; but you test him also for every kind of real work in civil life. You can find out the shirk, the weakling, the coward, the fool. You find them out, and find you cannot do good work with them, and then in varying degrees of capacity you find out the qualities of the other men until out of them you sift the natural leaders. In the battle or the campaign it is a mere truism to say that if you are going to do good work you have to measure the man by what he is, and not by anything adventitious connected with him. In the great campaigns, in the [-gxxxx-] tremendous battles of the civil war, or in the Philippine Swamps and jungles fighting an unseen adversary, you cared nothing as to whether the man on your right hand or on your left was rich or poor, what his social position had been, where his birthplace was, what the creed was according to which he worshipped his Maker. All that you could afford to pay heed to was the man's quality as a man[?.?] If he was the right type of man, if when the order was given to move he moved in the right direction, then you were for him, and if he was not you were against him. You would have been {inc apable|incapable} of the baseness of looking down upon him because he was not as well off, or of envying him and hating him because he was better off. You would have been incapable of either vice, and the two vices are equally bad. It is easy enough for some people to see that one of the vices is bad. They are both bad. The arrogance that looks down upon those who are less well off is as contemptible as, but not one whit more contemptible[+,+] than[+,+] the envy, the rancor, the mean jealousy, of hating the man who is better off. Each spirit

[pb 64 425:0358]

is utterly unworthy of the American citizen, utterly unworthy of the man brought up under our institutions. (Applause)

I plead with you, my fellow countrymen, to show in very fact in all our social, in all our industrial relations that same spirit shown by the men of the great civil war in their great work, shown, my comrades, by you in the lesser work which nevertheless you did so well. I believe in you and your future. I believe in the future of this great state, of this typical American state, I believe in the future of the American republic, because I am confident that in the long run the American people will conduct their government and will shape their social and {indus trial|industrial} systems along the [-[illegible]-] plain, {commonp|common} sense, simple lines of seeing that no man wrongs his fellow, of treating each man on his worth as a man, and of seeing to it that no encouragement be given to those who would stir up any feeling of hostility between brother and brother, whether that feeling takes the shape of hostility between section and section, or between class and class. I wish you well, and I believe in you with all my heart. (Great applause)
