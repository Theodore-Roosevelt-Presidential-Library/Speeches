Mr. Mayor; and you, My Fellow Citizens:

It is a great pleasure to have the chance of saying a word of greeting to you to-day. I have crossed the continent from the Atlantic to the Pacific. Now I am returning; and the thing that has struck me more than anything else on my journey is the essential unity of all Americans.

In being greeted by the men of the Grand Army, by the men who fought in the great civil war, I wish to say to them that they not only by what they did left us a union in name but in fact; for owing to what they did , now wherever an American President goes, {fron|from} the Atlantic to the Pacific, from the Gulf to the Canadian border, he [-xxxx-] feels absolutely at home, for he meets men in one place as in the other to whom he can appeal as Americans and as nothing else. The great lesson taught by the civil war was the lesson of brotherhood. You , my comrade/ who fought in the great war, you know that as you marched, as you did your work in camp, as you moved forward into battle, it made no difference as to the locality from which the man on your right hand or on your left hand came, as to what the creed was in accordance with which he worshipped his Maker, what [-xxxx-][+was+] his social standing or his means. You cared not [-xx xxx-] at all whether he were banker, bricklayer, business man, mechanic, or farmer, so long as he was a man, able to do his work in the world. So it is now in civil life as it was in military life then. The thing with which we have to concern ourselves is not the man's means, not his social position not the locality from which he comes, but the man's worth as a man. (Applause) The line of conduct , the line that divides decent citizens from those who are not, runs at right angles to the line of cleavage between occupation and occupation, between class and class, section and section, creed and creed. The really good American, the man who is loyal to the principles of our government as it was established, and the principles of our government as it was upheld by those who responded to the call of Abraham Lincoln, is the man who recognizes that fact as fundamental in our government. Of all the varieties of men who do ill to the country, the man who does wors[+t+] is the man who tries to {imflame|inflame} one set of Americans against another set of Americans on artificial grounds, whether he makes his appeal in the name of class against class, creed against creed, or section against section. [-[?That?]-][+The+] flag has pretty broad folds,
[pb 190 425:0521] and they can float over men of wide differences of opinion; but there is no room under its folds for the man who preaches either the gospel of arrogance or the gospel of envy and hatred toward any American. There are two sides to that question. It is a base and foul thing [-xx xxxx xxxx-] for one of our citizens to look down upon another because he is less well off; it is equally base, equally mean, equally un-American to look at him with envy, hatred, and rancor because he is better off. Either feeling is entirely unworthy of a people trained as we pride ourselves upon having been trained.

I am now coming from the Pacific east toward the Atlantic; and one [+thing+] that has interested me very much is to see how purely relative the terms east and west are. It does not make much difference whether a man lives east or west, north or south, if he is a straight American he is a pretty good fellow and other straight Americans can get on with him. (Cheers and applause)
