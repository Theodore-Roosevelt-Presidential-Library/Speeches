Mr. Senator, and you, my fellow citizens:

It is indeed a privilege to have the chance of addressing you this evening, and I have enjoyed greatly my trip through Iowa to-day. I began to speak at [-xx-] seven this morning, and as I think I have met about every man, woman and child within a reasonable radius of the railroads, I shall not detain you long this evening. In greeting you all I wish to express my heartfelt acknow[+l+][-l-]edgment of your turning out to see me. I know that you will not object to my saying a special word of greeting to the men of the Grand Army of the Republic[+,+] (Applause)[-b-][+B+]ecause, gentlemen, the rest of us owe the fact that there is a President to address you at all to what those men did; and they teach to us the lesson not merely of war but of peace, for mighty as was the triumph that they won in the years that closed at App[?o?]mattox, mightier still was the day when the great army disbanded and each man went back to his counting room, his shop or his farm, and took up again the work he had abandoned when the nation called him to arms. I am speaking in one of the chief manufacturing cities of Iowa. I am speaking also to those from round about who came from that rich agricultural country which takes in the entire state. I congratulate you upon your prosperity, upon your well-being. Something can be done, I think I may say that something has been done, by law to create and preserve that well-being. Something more can be done by the honest and faithful administration of the law; but most of all, gentlemen, such well-being depends upon the character of the average man. That is what counts in the long run. [margin: hand mark] (Applause ) It was just so in the war. We needed uniforms, needed guns and needed training, but if there was a man that had not the stuff in him you could not get it out of him. We won, and we won because the average man had the stuff in him, because he had the courage, because he had the iron will and dauntless resolution, because when he enlisted he saw it through. And [-there in-] this great state[-[?it?]-] has been built up not merely by the soil and the climate; it has been built up because there have been the right kind of men and women to take advantage of that soil and climate[+;+] [?Men?] and women who

[pb 163 425:0307]

were not afraid [-x-]ofwork, who were not seeking only the life of ease, the life of mere enjoyment, but were seeking to play their parts well in {thw|the} world. I have drawn more than one lesson already, friends and comrades, from your careers; let me draw another. When you went down because Abraham Lincoln called to arms you left the life of ease, you left the life of home, you left those that were dear to you, and you went off to spend the best years of your youth marching under the hot sun of the southern summer, marching when at noon the blanket felt too heavy and if you were a recruit you dropped it, and twelve hours afterwards one blanket was not enough. (Cries of "You know how it was"). I was there myself. Ours was only a little war; ours was not a big jo[?b?]; but we did it. (Applause.) And now you have left the memory of what you did as an imperishable heritage not only to your children and your children's children, but to all of us who have come after you and to the nation forever more. As you look back, what are the years of which you are proud? The years of ease? No. They were the years of effort, of toil, when you risked your lives, when you encountered wounds, when you faced fever cots in the hospital, when you saw the brightest and best shed their blood like water for the sake of a lofty ideal.

And as it was in war, so it is in peace. Look back, each man here, at the part of his life of which he is proudest to tell[-x-] his children. It is not the times that were eas[?i?]est, it is the times when the work was hard , but when he was man enough to do it. That is what you are proud of; that is what you are glad to tell your children of; that is what you hope to have your children emulate. Men and women of Iowa, I believe in you; I believe in the training you will give those that come after you, and I think I know that you will teach your children , in [-xxx-] turn not to shirk difficulties, not to try to go through life choosing the easiest course, but to choose the worthiest course and trample under foot any obstacle there is in the way. That is what counts. That is what makes a nation of men and of women.

I owe a peculiar debt of gratitude to Iowa, because I have taken one quarter of my cabinet from it. (Applause) [-[illegible]-]. I have been traveling all day with Secretary Shaw, my valued friend, and now I want to say a word of the Secretary who is absent -- Secretary Wilson. It was very fitting that we should have from the state of Iowa the Secretary of Agriculture, for I know [-xx xxxxx-] of no other

[pb 164 425:0307]

state in the union which has done more to develop the highest grade of farming, in which so much has been done both in the way of experimental work by the National Government andin the way of educational work by the state itself to make the farmers' profession one of such training, such practical and scientific skill, as to put it fairly beside any other profession in the entire country. It is a mere truism to say that on the welfare of the farmer and the wageworker rests the welfare of the entire state. If they do well we need not bother about the others, for the rest of us will do well also. Therefore in this city, a city of manufacturers, of business men, of wageworkers, in a state of farmers, I congratulate you upon having [-xxxx-] so well solved your share of the problems which confront this whole nation.

I do not have to do much preaching in Iowa. I think your practice sets a mark for my preaching. I do not have to preach when I {an|am} in the presence of the great war, excepting to preach to us younger fellows to remember that if ever the need comes we are to be held thrice shamed if, with the memory of your deeds before us, we fail to do our best in our turn so that you may feel that our homage to your memory is one of deeds and not from the lips only. (Applause) I do not have to do much preaching in Iowa as to the gospel of work. You have made Iowa what it is by work. I believe in play too; play for children; play for grown folks. Play when you play , but do not play while you work. (Applause) There is a time for it, but do not make the mistake of thinking it is the business of life. It is first-class recreation. I am glad to see any harmless enjoyment that any man or woman can get, only do not let it interfere with doing each his or her duty as the chance comes.

As I have passed through Iowa to-day -- and I have passed through it often before;[+—+] I know your people well-- I have been struck with the soil, with the climate, with the rich and fertile farms, with the prosperous and happy towns and cities; I have been struck by the high average of your citizenship. It has been a pleasure to me to see in the country districts how the electric cars, the telephone[+,+] the rural free delivery system, have joined in making less lonely the life of the farm[-xx-], in bringing it more and more toward an equality of pleasures with the life in the towns. I believe in your people, and admire them. I congratulate you upon your crops, your products, and I want to say that the thing that has pleased me most, with the possible exception of meeting the veterans,

[pb 165 425:0308]

as I came to-day[+,+] was meeting those at the other end of the line -- the children. I congratulate you upon all your crops, but especially upon your crop of children. (Laughter) They are all right in quality and they seem to be all right in [-xxxxxxxx-] quantity. I like your stock and I am mighty glad it is not dying out.

Men and women, my fellow citizens, my fe llow Americans, I shall not detain you longer. I have come simply to say a word of greeting to you, to thank you for coming to see me, to say that I believe in you with all my heart and soul, and that I wish for you in the future an even greater measure of prosperity and success than that you have so abundantly enjoyed in the past. (Great applause)
