Mr. {May or|Mayor}; Men and Women of Wisconsin:

[-Let [+me+] at the outset state to you that after listening to the vivid description of the Senator as to how I shoot and ride, I am bound that you shall never have the chance to see [-[?your?]-][+for+] yourselves. (Laughter) I [?fa[illegible]?] [+would+] rather that you should take it on his say so.-]

I am glad indeed to have the chance of coming through your great [-xxxx-] state, a state in the heart of the country, a state peculiarly typical of the great Republic in its diversity of occupation , its diversity of stock within its limits, and its absolute unity in patriotic feeling and purpose. (Applause)

I am sure that the rest of you whom I [-xxxxxx-] greet so heartily will not grudge my saying a word of special greeting to those to whom all Americans owe to more than to any other set of our people who ever have been on this con-[-tixnt-] tinent , the men who from '61 to '65 proved their truth by their endeavor whose conscience[-x-] and heart rang true on war's red touch-stone. (Applause) You, the men of the great war, taught us by your example what to do in war; and you, my comrades, the men of the lesser war, in '98, the men who went to Porto Rico, or Cuba, or the Philippines, speaking on behalf of you I want to say to the veterans that while we did not have a big job to do, we did it at any rate. (Applause) We in that war suffered from a complaint that you did not suffer from at all.--there was not enough war to go round. (Laughter and applause You did not have any such difficulty. You not only taught us the lessons of war, but you taught us the lessons of peace.

It is an awfully good thing for a man to be brought into contact with his fellow man on terms which are reduced down to the elemental basis of things. In ordinary life we are so apt to be divided by artificial distinctions. Our lives are so hemmed round that we often do not have the chance to test a man on his worth as a man. You who fought in the great war had to judge your comrades by the stuff there was in them. You remember the marches when at 10 {o(clock|o'clock} the blanket was too heavy, and if you were a raw recruit you threw it away,  and about twelve hours afterwards it was too light. You knew what it was to toil, footsore and weary, under the blazing heat of the southern sun; [pb 39 425:0106] you knew what it was to lie in the {trnches|trenches} in the frozen mud of winter;  you faced death by bullets, death on the fever cot of the hospital, you saw the brightest and the bravest around you shed their blood like water for the sake of an ideal;  you did all that and you knew what was the test you applied to the men around about. {?Little|Little} you cared whether they came from one state or another; little you cared what their creed was; {llittle|little} you cared whether their ancestors had come to this country two centuries and a half ago, or whether they themselves had been born on the other side, but came over here and prove[?d?] as you did by their valor , their loyalty to their adopted flag. You cared for none of those things. They were not the essentials. What you cared for was whether the man had the right qualit[?ies?], the right fiber in him. You wanted to know that when the order was given to move, he would move in the right direction. That [-ixxxk-] was what you were concerned with.

It is just the same thing in citizenship now. What we need ever to keep before us in this country, if we are to make , as [+we+] assuredly shall and will make, our scheme of government a success. What we need to keep ever before us is the fact that any distinction is artificial [-that-][+which+] divides one man from his fellow than by any other test than the man's real worth. (Applause) It is just as wicked, no matter from which standpoint the line of division is drawn;  whether it is from a standpoint of those who look down with arrogance upon the less well-off, or from the standpoint of those who [+regard+] with mean envy and rancor and hatred [-regard-] {thesewho|these who} are better off . In neither case is the feeling one worthy of the citizens of this great Republic, [-man-][+one+] worthy of the heirs of the spirit of Washington, of Lincoln, and of Grant. We have a right to demand that each man shall do his duty by his neighbor and his state. Beyond that it is not our affair. Let him manage his own private business as he wishes, so long as he infringes no right of any one else. Let him lead his private life as he desires . It is not our concern, provided [-[?only?]-][+only+] that he is a square and decent man, who [-xxxx-] wrongs no one and does his duty in peace and war;  and that is the common sense spirit of Americanism. (Applause)

That common sense spirit can be applied in more ways than one. When an appeal is made to any set of our people to do something wrong {in t heir|in their} own [pb 40 425:0106] interest . It would be well for them to remember what is always implied in the character of a man who asks you to do something that is not quite straight, when he says it will be for your benefit. [-Let me tell you a short story. A number of years ago I, myself, lived far west of here, out on the plains -- a cow puncher-- (and by the way it is curious what [-x-] relative terms east and west are. I lived out on the Little Missouri in those days, and at the end of one season one of the punchers came to me and said "Boss, I would like my time". I said "What are you going to do". He said "I am going to spend the winter in the far east." I said what do you mean by the far east, Norway or Nubia" ?. He said, "No, Duluth." Duluth represented to him the most eastern verge of the horison.) You know in a cow country in those days, and to a slight extent still, there were no fences, and the cowboy and the branding iron took the place of them. And the way you keep up your herd was that the calf was [-brandedxwith-] branded with the brand of the cow[+/+] Maverick's [+were unbranded+] yearlings or  well grown calves ; and people of not over-sensitive honesty would put a brand on them that did not [?indicate?] the cow. One day a cow puncher with whom I was out riding when we [?struck?] a cow and a pretty well grown calf, he got down his rope and tied it down;  and then we took the cinch ring [+made a fire+] and heated it , and he started to run [-x-] on the brand. I said "Hello! you are putting on my brand, this is a Thistle cow." He said "That is all right". I said "I do not know about that, what do you mean?". He said "I always run on the bosses brand". I said "Oh,I see; now come back to the ranch and get your time." He said "I was running on your brand." I said "Yes, but if you will steal for me you will steal from me." that is a pretty safe rule to go on . If a man will do something crooked and ask you to back him on the ground that it will turn out to your advantage, he will do something crooked to your harm if the chance comes.-]

In public life and in private life no country can {affrd|afford} in the long run to tolerate aught but a standard of absolute honesty, of fair {delaing|dealing} as between man and man, neighbor and neighbor;  and in the long run the most useful [-kind-] kind of politics is the kind of politics that teaches each man and set of men to demand justice, to be satisfied with no less than justice, but to do justice also. (Applause) So it is, gentlemen,  in the field of international politics. Our Republic has to take a great place in the world. It cannot help [pb 41 425:0107] it. We have had very worthy citizens during the past few years who have felt reluctant about our Republic going out into the world to do its duty. It {cann ot|cannot} be helped. If you are a big nation you have got to play a big nation's part. You can play it badly if you want to, but you have got to play it well or badly, one way or the other, and I think I know you too well not to desire to see it played it well. Let us play it manfully, but courteously and fairly. Let us scrupulously refrain from wronging the weak,and see to it that we are not wronged by the strong. Let us remember that boasting, speaking ill of our neighbors is just as offensive in {an nation|a nation} as in an individual. Here in your own community, among your own friends,  on the farms, in the shops, in business, in all the ways of life, you despise a man who cannot take his own part. At least you ought to despise him, and I hope you do. If a man cannot take his own part, it is not worth taking. And you will a good deal more than despise him if he oppresses others, and you dislike him as well as  despise him if he is quarrelsome, and always speaking ill of his {neigh bors|neighbors}, and inviting trouble.

It is just the same {wasin|was in} international affairs. I want to see our public men and our writers in the press make the point of speaking courteously of foreign nations, of refraining from any expression that will invite trouble, of remembering that nobody likes to have {disagreeab e|disagreeable} things said of him, that this country is growing so big, {t hat|that} what its people say is read abroad, and that therefore it is wise and it is decent not to use {langage|language} that will hurt other peoples' feelings upon any question of our foreign policy. Let me quote {a gain|again} my favorite proverb[+:+] "Speak softly and carry a big stick; you will go far." (Applause.) Remember that this country wants peace[+.+] We are honorably desirous of peace with all the nations of mankind. We wish them well. Let us speak courteously of them . Let us treat them with the spirit of scrupulous[-x-], fair {deaking|dealing}, do everything  we can to avoid trouble, and then keep ourselves in such shape that it will be mighty poor policy for any one to have trouble with us. [?Now?] is not that sound common sense? (Cries of "Yes"., applause, cheers)

In closing[+,+] just let me say one word of thanks to all of you for coming out to greet me; to you veterans of the great {w ar|war}, to my comrades who furnished the guard to-day;let me here have the chance of thanking[+,+] as I will not have the [pb 42 425:0107] {cha nce|chance} {oth erwise|otherwise}, the members of the local Brotherhood of Engineers for the greeting they [-sent me,-][+conveyed+] and [-for-] the [+{buqquet|bouquet} of+] flowers they sent me[+ through their representative, Mr. [illegible]+] [-I appreciate peculiarly-]. We all of us are under a debt to the railroad men -- among other things for the fact {th t|that} they furnished one of the members of the Anthracite Strike Commission, whose {rport|report} I feel marks as good a bit of work as  has been done in our country., towards settling some of the gravest problems that as a nation we have before us. (Applause) Let me thank all of you, and say how glad I am to see you, and in particular how {g lad|glad} I have been to see the children. They seem to be all right in quality and all right in quality, and as I think {vety|very} highly of you, I should be mighty sorry if I thought the stock was dying out. (Great applause)
