Mr. Mayor; and you, My Fellow Citizens; Men and Women of Wyoming:

It is indeed a {ple asure|pleasure} to be here in your beautiful state on this beautiful day. I do not feel like a stranger among you. It {istwenty|is twenty} years since I first came into Wyoming. At that time I was in the cow business myself. (Applause) I know your people well. The Mayor just said to me he was glad I [-am-][+was+] in sympathy with the west. I told him that sympathy was {thewrong|the wrong} word; that I did not sympathize with the west; I admired it.

Here in this state I am particularly glad to have the chance of speaking from the State University building. Nothing has delighted me more during the twenty-five years that I have known the west than to see the rapidity with which together with the material development of the country hand in hand with it has gone the building up of the opportunities for leading a higher life, without which material development by itself must always count for so little.

Before going on I want to thank the Black Hills Lodge of the Locomotive Firemen for the gift tendered through the Mayor from them to me and to say that I wish I were able to thank them in person. For the last two months and for the next week I have owed and shall owe every hour and every day my safety to the care of the railroad men. I have appreciated deeply all that they have done for me, and appreciate very greatly the spirit that prompts them to remember me in the fashion they have.

I want also to say a special word of greeting on this day of all others, on Memorial Day, to the veterans of the Grand Army of the Republic, the men to whom we owe it that there is to-day a country [-xx xxx-] at all, and that to-day the President of the United States can go across this continent from the Atlantic to the Pacific, can go from the Gulf to the border line that separates us on the north from ou[-t-][+r+] Canadian brothers, and everywhere find himself at home, everywhere find himself among his own people. After all there is but little I can preach that you cannot yourselves learn from what these men of the Grand Army have practiced; and it is practice that counts[-[?;?]-]. Precept is very good; but one ounce of performance is worth a ton of promise and exhortation. The qualities which I admire {innthe|in the} west, the {qualitieswhich|qualities which} have built up this commonwealth and her sister commonwealths here, are the qualities which to so preeminent a degree these men of the civil war showed in the great and dreadful days from
[pb 178 425:0515] '61 to '65. I could {saylittle|say little} in the way of advice to the students, the undergraduates of this college, in addition to asking them in their turn as their lives open before them to do as you did, you who sprang to arms {whea|when} Abraham Lincoln called. [-They-][+You+] showed the qualities that we have to show in citizenship -- qualities of a two-fold type[-x-] In the first place, the quality of disinterested devotion to an ideal, the quality that makes a man at one time a good man in the home, a good neighbor, a good friend, a square man to deal with, a good man to work along side of -- that is the quality that makes a man appreciate his responsibility to his fellows. If you had not had that quality -- the quality which in one of its [+highest+] manifestations we call patriotism, love of country -- if you had {hot|not} had that [-quality-] naught else would have availed. It is in civil life as it is in military life. If a man {nas|has} not got the root of righteousness in him, the spirit of honesty, of decency, of fair play, of fair dealing, then all the ability, all the courage and strength he may have, but makes him the more dangerous. If the man is not a decent man first, then whatever else is added to him in the way of strength or courage is mere[+ly+] added to him for evil. In the civil war you knew that[-[?,?]-] the man who did not have the power of loyalty to his comrades, loyalty to his regiment, loyalty to the flag, loyalty to the nation -- if he did not have that the stronger and abler he was the worse he was, the more dangerous he was. (Applause)

As a foundation for character we must have that spirit of decency; and above all, men and women of this republic, we must have it in the republic. The stream will not rise higher than the source. The government cannot in the long run, in a government of the people, be better than the average of the people; and therefore every American citizen who falls below the level to which he should rise in morality and decency, sins against the state and the nation as well as against himself. (Applause) You have got to have that first; and it is not enough. In addition thereto[+,+] as you well know, [-[?you?]-] who fought in the great civil war, [-in addition thereto-] you must have other qualities. No matter how patriotic a man [-wa-] [+was in the army+] [-xxxxxxxxxx-], if he ran away he was no good. ({Applamse|Applause}) You wanted a good man; but you wanted a man who would"stay put" when the time came. So in civil life, the cloistered virtue that sits at home, especially if it takes its virtue out in bemoaning how bad other people are[+,+] [-that virtue-] never yet helped a man or helped a nation. What you need in addition to decency and morality is the spirit
[pb 179 425:0516] that makes a man able to hold his own among men, the spirit of courage, of hardihood, of energy, of resolution, the spirit that dares, that endures, that does, the spirit that recognizes in an obstacle not something from which to flinch, but something to overcome, something which can be made a steppingstone to triumph. We needed that in war, and we need it in peace. We need of course to have in us the lift toward lofty things, which makes a man capable in[-[?a?]-] time of need of heroic action; but the heroic action is only called for occasionally, and the capacity to take such action is in no wise a substitute for failure to perform the ordinary, everyday {duti es|duties}; and it is the ordinary, everyday duties which tend[-[?s?]-] to make a man capable of doing greater deeds when the chance occurs.

Let me again draw an example from the experience [-xxx-] of those {me n|men} of the civil war. You know that the man you wanted near you was not the excellent gentleman who wished to be a hero, but meanwhile did not care to dig kitchen sinks. You wanted the man who when it was his business to police the camp, policed it; who when it was his business to march, marched; when the time of fighting came[+,+] that could take care of itself. If he did his [-xxxx-] other work up to that time he[-xxxxxxxx xx xx-] would be apt to fight well too. In {civi l|civil} life we need to have the quality that in a great crisis enabled a nation to rise level to the crisis. The way to acquire as a nation that quality is to do day by day the work that our hands find to do on that day. To but few of us comes the chance ever to do anything noted and out of the way. Since the foundation of the republic the only generation which as a generation had that chance was the generation that sent you, the men of the Grand Army, to battle. But that does not excuse the rest of us from doing our share of work as the work arises.

I wish to say one word specially to the students, graduates, and undergraduates of this university. I am particularly pleased to be here and to speak to you, because so much of the real greatness of this state and of sister states ought to and will depend upon how you and those like you who have had your advantages play your part in after life. You have had here and are having here in this {univeristy|university} training in practical affairs, training also in what in the old days used to be called the humanities. You have the chance to train yourselves in many different branches of study. That training confers no special privilege upon you; but it does put upon you an added burden of responsibility. The state {ha s|has} given you something for which you are in honor bound in your after lives
[pb 180 425:0516] as men and as women to make a return. Any man who receives anything without making a return for it puts himself in an unpleasant position, in a position such as no man of our country ought to put himself. When you take from the state the higher education the state gives, you are in honor bound to repay the obligation in the only way in which you can repay it -- by disinterested, intelligent service to the [-x-] state as a citizen afterwards. (Applause) The state gives the training and the education, and the state has a right to expect its return in the standard of citizenship which you set to others and up to which you live yourselves.

My friends, it has been a great pleasure to catch this glimpse of you this morning. I believe in you with all my heart; I believe in the future of this state[+[?,?]+] because I believe in the quality of the men and women who make it up. The government can do something. Nothing has pleased me more than having been able to do my part in getting the national government to put upon its statute books that law which [-xxx-] in its importance to our home development I think surpasses any other law enacted for many years -- the national irrigation law. ({Applasue|Applause}) We have made a beginning now in having the national government do its part in developing the possibilities of irrigated agriculture, of irrigation as an adjunct to stockraising here in the Rocky Mountain states and the states of the great plains. The government can do something; it can do a good deal; but after all, it can only supplement the work of the individual citizens. The government can give the chance; but it is the man or the woman who has got to {takeadvantage|take advantage} of the chance. Nothing that the law can do, nothing that natural advantages can do, will ever take the place of the sum of those qualities in the average man which in their aggregate we know by the name of character. I believe in your future; I believe in the future of our common country; because I believe that the average citizen has in him that quality of decency, that quality of courage[+[?,?]+] [-and [?courage?]-] and furthermore that quality of common sense for the lack of which no brilliance or genius in individual or nation can atone, and because of which I believe this nation will go forward in the century now opening until it stands as no {rppublic|republic} has ever yet stood upon which the sun has shone.

I want to have the pleasure of introducing to you that member of my Cabinet whose concern it has been to do all that in him lies for the development [-xx xxx xxxxxxx-] within our country of the tiller of the soil, the stockraiser, [-xxx-], [-x xx xxxxxxxx xx-] the man who makes his living in the oldest and I am inclined to say healthiest manner -- I introduce to you the Secretary of Agriculture, Mr. Wilson. (Cheers and applause)
