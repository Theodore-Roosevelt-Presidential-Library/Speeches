My Fellow Citizens:

I count myself fortunate in being given the privilege of dedicating this university. We are all as Americans to be congratulated that there are among us men who so worthily apply the fortunes that they have made[+,+] in devoting a portion of their fortunes to the cause of education in this country.

Mr. Millikin, I feel that as an American it is proper for me to express to you and to those like you the obligation that good Americans feel for what you and they have done in this university and in other educational institutions throughout the land. (Applause) I am especially pleased that I am to take part in [-xxxx-] the dedication of an institution of learning where so much teaching is to be with a direct view to the industrial betterment of the community. Ours is an age of specialization; and the man who is to do the highest industrial work will find himself {imme asurably|immeasurably} better prepared for it, if he can have [-[?had?]-] the proper kind of industrial training. I congratulate all of the men and women whom I address because this building consecrated to such a purpose is being erected here; for not only does such an institution benefit the student, benefit those supposed to be the only [-be-]beneficiaries, but it also indirectly helps all of us; because it becomes at once a center for the diffusion [-[?of learning, the diffusion?]-] of education.

Now I wish to say a word to the students, to those who will profit by what has been done. To whom much has been given, from him much shall rightly be expected. The man who has received an education which he owes in part either to the state or to private gift, has accepted a favor which {aw|as} a self-respecting American citizen he is bound to return; and he can return it in but one way. There is no return he can make to his alma mater of a tangible kind. There is no return he can make in the way of physical repayment of what has been spent upon his education either to the state or to the [-xxxxx-] individual donor, to the benefactor of the university. The return must be made in the shape of good citizenship in the community. The return for the education must take the form [?of?] the highest possible achievement on the part of the man who has received the [?education?]. The college man is entitled to no privileges because he has [?received?] a college education; but he is to be held peculiarly accountable , to be [pb 61 425:0554] held to have acquired to a peculiar degree new responsibilities. The boy to whom his parents by their thrift and saving, or to whom the state by its erection of public schools, high schools, normal schools, universities, or to whom outside {bemefactors|benefactors} through the erection of schools of learning, have given the [?chance?] to acquire special training[+,+] to fit him in the struggle for life[+,+] is bound to show that he has in him the quality to make good use of what has been given to him. Exactly as we rightly hold the man who has had a good training and advantages at home as one on whom we have a right to call for the proper performance of duty both in his home and with reference to the state, so we have that same right to demand an extra quality of service from the boy or the girl, the young man or the young woman, who has had the benefit of training, general or technical, in an institution such as this. In our American life no man is entitled to receive what he does not in some shape make return for; and the student, the college graduate, must make the return for the training that he has received in the shape of good citizenship in the community at large. (Applause)

Let me say to you, men and women of Illinois, that in traveling through your state, much as I {ammimpressed|am impressed} by the boundless beauty and fertility of the state, much though I am impressed by all that nature and Providence have done for you and by the way in which you have taken advantage of the gifts thus put at your disposal, I am even more impressed by the way in which you have recognized that you have more than merely material needs for which to care. It is of course the merest truism to say that natural advantages count only so far as they are made of use by the average citizen; and the success or failure of our American government in the future depends upon what kind of men and women the boys and girls of to-day turn out. I congratulate you upon the evidences I have seen that you appreciate that fact; that you are training in your homes, in your schools, in colleges such as this, the American boy and girl of to-day to turn into the kind of man, the kind of woman, to-morrow, who will be [-xxx-] enabled to carry on with unfaltering courage and strength the work that the Americans of the past have done.

We have great problems before us as a nation. We have solved great problems in the past, and we can do our duty in the future only on condition that we [pb 62 425:0554] breed the same kind of man and woman that we have had in the past. Illinois sprang to the front and made the wonderful record it did in the civil war, because Illinois had the right stuff of citizenship within its borders. (Cheers and applause) And I believe that the people of Illinois; I believe that the people of all this country, will rise level to the needs of the future, because I believe we will continue to train properly the right type of citizenship to do the duties of the future. (Cheers and applause).
