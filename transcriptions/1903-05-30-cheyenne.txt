Senator, Governor, Mr. Mayor; and you, Men and Women of Wyoming, My Fellow Americans:

It is indeed a great pleasure to be with you this afternoon; to be greeted by the {pepresentatives|representatives} of the {w ageworkers|wageworkers}, by the representatives of the business interests, by the men of the mines, the ranch, the men who are building up the industrial fabric of this state. It is especially pleasant to be greeted by the children. You know I have strong views about children, and I am delighted [-xx-] [+that+] the output in Wyoming seems to be all right in quality and quantity. In greeting all of you, with thanks, I know that you expect me to say that my chief and most heartfelt greeting on this day of all the year is reserved for the men who in the mighty days proved their truth by their endeavor, whose metal rang true on war's red touchstone -- the veterans of the great war; (Applause ) the veterans of the great war from '61 to '65. With them I include also , and I know with their assent, the men , my comrades, of the lesser war; [-[?In?]-] that war in which this nation needed to show but the merest fraction of its giant strength, and yet [-the-][+a+] contest [-fought-][+fraught+] with [-the-] immense consequences to the future of our republic and to the world as a whole. I know also that you, the representatives of the volunteers, will most gladly join with me when I say a word of special greeting to the men who make it their life business to preserve untarnished the honor of the American flag -- the officers and enlisted men of the army and navy of the United States. ({Applasue|Applause})

You of the great civil war left us not only a reunited {cuntry|country}, left us not only a flag made whole and without a seam, but you left us likewise forever as a heritage of honor through all time the memory of the great deeds by {whch|which} the country was kept united, and the lessons to be observed in peace no less than in war that you taught by your practice and example in the great struggle. It is a fitting thing to be introduced on Decoration Day by a United States Senator who not only served in the great civil war, but to whom it was given to win the highest treasure that come[+s+] to any American soldier -- the medal of honor for distinguished gallantry on stricken fields. (Applause) We of the present time, whose lines are cast in pleasant places, who are the inheritors of what you of the great war won, can best show our loyalty to you and to those like you who fought victorious
[pb 182 425:0517] in that war, not by praise alone but by the way we shape our lives so that you need never have cause to feel that we are unworthy of you. You left us lesson after lesson just as important to be applied in civil as in military Life. You left us the great lesson of brotherhood, the applied lesson of brotherhood. Of all wars in history this was one of the strangest, for you, the victors, by the fact of your victory left us in the relation of brothers to the gallant vanquished . You made not only the side for which you fought, but even more the side against which you fought, your [-[?debate?]-][+debtor+] forever by the victory. You left us also the right as Americans to feel pride in the valor shown by every American whether he wore the blue or the gray in that struggle[+,+] who did his duty as the light was given [-xx-] him to[-o-] see it. (Cheers and applause) One of the things that ha[-s-][+ve+] always made me feel proudest of my countrymen, proudest of the men of the great war, is the fact that in a reunion of the veterans of the Grand Army [-there-] is the very place where I am sure to meet the most cordial response when I pay a tribute to the valor of the men who fought against you in the civil war. ({Applasue|Applause}) You left us furthermore the lesson of brotherhood. Each of you when you got into the army after you had seen a campaign or two realized how absolutely essential it was to strip off the tinsel [-xx-] [+from+] a man, to strip off the non-essentials, and judge him by his worth as a man. There never was a contest where the victory more absolutely resulted from the resolute determination of our people and of that mighty man of the people -- Abraham Lincoln -- to treat each man on his worth as a man. (Cheers and applause) Each of you as he moved forward into battle was immensely interested in the man on his right and the man on his left. It is a comfortable thing, comrades, if you are going forward to feel that the man on each side goes forward too. It is always pleasant to feel that when the order comes to move he will move in the right way. You were concerned in that. You did not care a snap of your finger where that man's birthplace had been; you did not care a snap of your finger what was the creed in accordance with which he worshipped his Maker; you did not care in the least what [-[?were?]-][+was+] his wealth or his social position, whether a banker, a bricklayer, a lawyer, a mechanic, a farmer; it did not make any difference so long as he would "stay put". That is what you wanted to know. (Applause)

It is the same thing now in civil life. We cannot afford if we are to make this republic true to its promise[+,+] if we are to make its mighty future as essen[pb 183 425:0518]tially great as its past,[+[?--?]+] we cannot afford as citizens to sunder in the deeper matters along lines other than the line of conduct which separates good citizens from bad citizens. There are good and bad in every class, in every creed, in every section, in every occupation. [-and-] We are false to the principles which Washington and his people [+sought to crystallize+] into government; we are false to the principles of Abraham Lincoln and those who held up his hands during [-the four dreadful years from '61 to '65-] the four dreadful and ever glorious years from '61 to '65.[+[?,?]+] if we substitute any other test for the test of the man's worth as a man. If a man is a decent citizen, if he does his duty by his {neighors|neighbors}, by his family, by the state, if he is just and fair in his dealings with his fellows, he has the right to challenge the good-will and the support of all his fellow citizens. The man who denies it because he is either richer or poorer, because he belongs to one creed or to another, because he lives in one section or in another, because he comes in one category of wealth or employment rather than in another, the man who denies the just treatment to be accorded to the citizen who himself behaves justly, is himself false to the principles of American citizenship. (Applause

I appeal with whatever force I may have for the practical {applicatioj|application} in civil and social life of those principles of brotherhood for which many of your comrades, you men of the civil war, laid down their lives; and for which each of you risked his life in those days. You have taught us other lessons. You have taught us how to apply the law of brotherhood. You have taught us also what is of real value and what is of little value in life. You have taught us to have a proper sense of the relative proportion of things. I would preach to my countrymen not the life of ease, not the effortless life of comfort, of avoidance of risk, of {avoindance|avoidance} of trouble. I would ask them not to strive to find out the things that are easiest to do, but the things that are best worth {doi ng|doing}. In '61 when Abraham Lincoln called to arms, did you do the easy thing? No, not a bit! The easy thing was to let some other man go to the war; the easy thing was to stay at home, to live softly and comfortably, to try to go about your own business, to try to get material benefits for yourself. You chose the better part. Your choice was for effort, and toil, and hardship. You chose to be numbered among those who were to struggle upward and onward over the rugged slopes of greatness. You chose to spend your days in the well-nigh intolerable fatigue of marching under the southern sun, the marches when as you remember as recruits you found about
[pb 184 425:0518] midday the blanket so heavy you tended to throw it off and about midnight you wished there were two of them. You chose the life that meant shivering night after night through the black midwinter hours in the frozen mud of the trenches. You chose the risk of battle, the risk of death by disease, of death on the fever cots of the hospitals. You did it, and did it gladly; because you had in you the lift that lofty souls feel, the lift of generous enthusiasm for a high ideal.

What are the things in all your life, what is the time in that life, that you look back to with most profound satisfaction, and the memory of which you will hand on as a heritage of honor not only to your children and your children's children, but to all your countrymen throughout the ages as long as our country lasts? What you hand on is the memory of the days of effort, of toil, of danger, not the memory of the days that were peaceful and prosperous, not the memory of the days when life was easy, when life was soft, when there was no effort needed, when no trials were to be encountered.

It is just so in civil life. We do not now have to face the trials faced by those who fought in the great civil war, by those who upheld the statesmanship of Lincoln, and made good the generalship of Grant and Sherman; but each man here, each woman here, is brought face to face continually with the choice whether he or she will take the easy course or take the course which leads to hard work worth doing. Life is not easy. Life is hard. [-xxxx-] It is always hard to any one who thinks seriously about it, who tries to do his duty, but with halting, stumbling, with many short-comings, but who still tries to do his duty in the world. I would preach to you the doctrine[+,+] not of trying to lead an easy life. I would not for one moment tell you that anything you could do, my fellow citizens, will always make your lives easy. On the contrary, if you have in you the lift toward lofty things you will be impelled into the stony roads where you stumble, but where you walk upwards toward greatness, toward the life worth living. It is easy enough to avoid trouble for the present. You generally insure the larger portion of it for the future; but you can generally avoid it for the present if you are built that way. You men of Wyoming are making of this commonwealth one of the commonwealths of which all our people will be proud, because you are not built that way, because you have had it in you to desire to strive, to struggle, for material success, and for that life which is built upon material well-being You here in the west more than any other section of our people have realized in actual practice
[pb 185 425:0519] [-xxxx-] the virtues illustrated to us by the conduct of the men who fought in the great civil war.

I believe in you, men and women of Wyoming; I believe in those like you; and I believe in the future of this country ; because I believe that your average citizenship is such that this country during the century opening will choose to tread the path of duty and of greatness, rather than of mere ease. We have great problems that confront us within and without. We are not to be excused if we shrink from them, {of ir|or if} with selfish timidity we say we will not have to settle them in our day, let those that come after us settle them. Each one of us is his brother's keeper; each one of us is the keeper of his son; each one of us is the keeper of the generations that are to come after us. We must in honor strive so to handle ourselves that when [-the-] those generations arise they will find that we have taken the right steps in beginning the solution of the problems that [-were-][+are+] to confront them as they confront us. We must take them in a spirit of courage, a spirit of love, and also in a spirit of common sense. We will make but a poor fist of it if we try to deal with the social problems of to-day and leave out the element of love, of brotherhood; and we will make just as poor a fist of it if we leave out the saving grace of common sense.

This nation in dealing with [-with foreign-] affairs with [-xxxxx-] [+foreign+] nations should follow just the rule that we regard as right for a private citizen. If there was one man who in my day, and I guess now, was not respected in the west, it was the man who talked, [-xxx-] [+and+] boasted, and threatened; and when the pinch came did not make good. It is just so with our nation. I never want to see us bluster or play the bully. We should be [-xxxx-] as scrupulous to refuse to [-xxxxxx-] [+oppress+] the weak as to hold our own against the strong. (Applause) We should be careful not to speak insultingly of others, not to taunt them, not to provoke to war. But when we have {madeup|made up} our mind that a certain policy is demanded by the interest and honor of our nation, then we should announce that as our policy and be prepared to make that policy good. (Applause) In other words, act {inaccordance|in accordance} with the proverb I heard in the old days, when I myself lived in the cow country. The proverb ran, Don't draw unless you mean to shoot. I ask that we apply that nationally. I believe, for instance, in the Monroe doctrine with all my heart. I believe we should be prepared to back that doctrine up to any extent if it [-[?become?]-][+becomes+] necessary. The only way it can be done is by building an efficient navy, {butconstantly|but constantly}
[pb 186 425:0519] building and keeping in the best possible condition in sea practice such magnificent battleships as the W[?y?]oming, the ship named after your own state.

My friends and fellow citizens, I have only to thank you for your reception, to say how glad I have been [+to be+] here to-night, and to say good bye, especially to you, the men of the great war; and on behalf of all our other citizens, I as one of of them, [-on their behalf,-] pledge you that we shall try in the coming years to rise level to the high standard which you set for evermore as the standard of our national life. (Cheers and applause)
