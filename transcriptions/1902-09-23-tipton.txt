Mr. Chairman; and you, my fellow Americans:

In no way could I be more pleased than in being introduced as you, Mr. Chairman, have introduced me, for there are things a thousand fold more important than any party division, -The essential and un[+d+]erlying bond of unity among all decent Americans who try to act square and fair as citizens of our common country. And I am particularly glad to have the chance of seeing the school children before me, and as is always the case when I stop here in Indiana[+,+] [-at-][+of+] seeing also in evidence the men who in the dark days from '61 to '65 put into practice under the greatest stress the principles that decent men must preach, and I would appeal to their practice rather than to the preaching of me or of any one else. Now, gentlemen, as I said, we can afford to have party differences. The great point that we cannot afford to be without is good citizenship, decent citizenship. Whatever the man's opinions may be upon the less important questions, on the most vital and fundamental questions we must stand alike. There isn't any patent device for getting good citizenship. There is no particular law or scheme of laws by which it is possible to create [-xx-] in any man those fundamental virtues which we mean when we speak of him as a good citizen. Laws do something, but it is the man behind the law that counts, just exactly as my comrade over there, you of the big war, when you went down , you and your fellows who followed Grant or Sherman or Thomas or Sheridan, you had different arms from the arms carried by the men who followed Washington in the Revolutionary War; you were [-[?drill?]-] differently uniformed; you were drilled by different tactics, but the spirit that drove you to victory was the same. Our people triumphed at Appomattox because they had in them the stuff that wrought out[+ the+] triumph of Yorktown in the Revolutionary War. And now your younger comrades who have been in the Philippines, and who by their deeds there have wrought so mighty a work for our people and for civilization, they were engaged in a war trivial compared to that in which you were. They had different [?arms;?] they had the smokeless powder, small caliber, high power rifle; they [pb 63 424:0750] were drilled in open order tactics, but they had the same spirit in them, and that is why they won honor and renown for our people. We need good weapons. Take a good man with a poor weapon and put him up against a good man with a good weapon, and of course the latter will win; but take the best weapon made and give it to a fool or a coward and a good man will beat him with a club. [-Laughterxxxxx-] [-(A voice in crowd, [illegible] "[?You are all right Teddy?]")-] And it is the same thing with our laws here in this country. We have made this nation what it is partly because of the constitution, because of the fundamental law of the land; because of the legislation that we have had; but not merely because of that. There have been other countries with our system of government, [+a+] system of government sometimes copied from ours; a constitution that was a beautiful constitution on paper; laws that were excellent on the statute books; but those countries have almost failed absolutely in the struggle for self government, because they did not have the right type of men behind the law. We need good laws but we need still more to have back of the law the high character of individual citizenship. There is no royal road to being a good citizen; least of all is there any patent device by which through some law you can make a man a good citizen any more than you can make him prosperous by a law. A law can give him the opportunity to do the best that is in him, and if it is a bad law it can mar his chances for accomplishing anything, but it cannot make him succeed. He must have the stuff in him or you cannot get it out of him. You need good citizenship. [-[illegible]-] there is more than one element that enters into good citizenship.—In the first place and fundamentally, honesty,[-andx-] decency. I care not how able a man is[+,+] if he does not have in him the sense that makes him respond to the moral law,then the abler he is the more dangerous he is. In the Revolutionary War one of the ablest and most brilliant soldiers we had, the man who fought so gallantly in Canada, the man who with distinguished courage led our forces on the [-x-] field at Saratoga and was there wounded seriously; that man with all of his gallantry, all of his courage did not have the root of righteousness in him, and so his name-the name of Benedict Arnold is a hissing [pb 64 424:0750] and [+a+] by-word among the nations forevermore. Exactly as it is with the soldier, if he lacks patriotism [-x-], loyalty to the flag, it will make him more dangerous to the country, so the abler a citizen is, the worse he is for the community if he has not got [-xx-] in him the sense that makes him cling to the right and forswear the wrong. Then you need for good citizenship,first, honesty, decency; and that is not enough. I do not care how honest a man is[+,+] if he is timid he is of very small use. In the civil war you needed men who would be spurred by patriotism; but it made mighty little difference how patriotic [-xx-] a man was if in time of action he ran away. You needed to have him"stay put" in the first place. So in citizenship you need honesty as the prerequisite, but it is not enough. With honesty must go courage[+,+] hand in hand with it, If out of the two you are going to get anything approaching good citizenship. Even honesty and courage are not enough. No matter how honest a man is, no matter how brave he is, if [-[illegible]-] he has been born foolish you can do but little with him. We need honesty, we need courage, and in addition [-xx-] to that we need the saving grace of common sense. [-Mxxxxxxxxx-] Hardness of heart is a dreadful quality. In the long run I do not know that it does much more damage than softness of head. (Laughter.) You need in order to get really good work out of any community that the average citizen of that community should have beaten into him the qualities which you are now training up your children to have, the qualities of honesty, of fair dealing as between man and man, the qualities of courage, courage physical and courage moral, and finally with honesty, with courage[+,+]to be taught the vital need of the old humdrum workaday indispensable quality of common sense. (Applause.)
