Senator Dolliver; and you, My Fellow Americans; Men and Women of Iowa:

{Iy|It} is a great pleasure to have the chance of saying a word of greeting to you this morning. I have come from a trip to and fro across the continent; and I want to say that [+of+] all things[+,+] the thing that has struck me most on that trip is the essential unity of our people. A good American is a good American anywhere in this land. (Cheers and applause) I do not think that until one has traveled a little one gets a real idea of how purely relative a matter east and west is. I cannot say what a pleasure it has been to go from the Atlantic to the Pacific and find everywhere men and women to whom I could appeal in the name of the same ideals, and who were responsive ever to that appeal. We owe that especially to the men who in '61 when Abraham Lincoln called answered the call. (Applause)

In greeting all our people I wish to greet especially those to whom we owe it that we now have a common country, that we now have a country through which a President can travel to meet his countrymen. (Applause) These men, the men of '61 to '65, by what they did not only established the Union, not only left us as a heritage of honor forever more the deeds they did, but they left us the memory of how those deeds were done, the memory of the spirit in which they were done. They taught us for all time the two great lessons -- the lesson of appreciating what is the really important in life, [-and-][+the+] lesson of brotherhood. It is not important to have an easy time. It is highly unimportant to try to live a life of mere pleasure. It is vitally important to see what is worth doing and then to do it at[-x-] any cost.

Here to-day as everywhere throughout this Union and in every meeting of Americans you, the men of the civil war, are given the places of honor for ever and always;and your deeds shall live to be told by our children's children and on through generation after generation as long as there shall be a country to have a recorded history on this continent. Why? Because in '61 and the years following you chose not the easy [-xxxx-][+path+], but the path that led across [-xxx-] the stony slopes of greatness to the goal of triumph for the flag and the nation. (Applause) When Lincoln called[+,+] the easy
[pb 10 425:0528] thing was not to [?answer?] the call. You did not choose the life of comfort; you did not choose the life which was easy; you did not walk delicately in the earth's soft places; you did not pay heed to your own material well-being; on the contrary, the men of the civil war {abandonedfor|abandoned for} the time that they were in battle the hope of all material gain. They faced hardship and risk; they took up willingly {thelife|the life} that meant {sufferingby|suffering by} cold in the winter nights, suffering by [-xxxxx-] heat in the summer days on the marches; the practical experience of grinding fatigue, of hunger, the ever-present chance of death in battle, death on the fever cots of the hospitals. They did all that gladly because they had in them the lift toward lofty things that goes with generous souls; because they had in them the spirit that bade them distinguish between the things that [?are?] unessential and the things that are [-un-]essential in life. It is unessential to have an easy time. It is vitally essential to do well your duty, to do well the things worth doing. These men had it in them to see what was essential. That is one lesson they taught us.

The other is the lesson of brotherhood; the recognition of each man as a man, of seeing what was important in his character, and disregarding [-xxxx-] the trivial. [+To+] Each one of you as you moved forward into battle, it mattered a great deal whether the man on your right hand or your left had the right stuff in him. That was of consequence. You wanted to know if when he moved he would in the right way. It was of absolutely [-[illegible]-] no consequence what the creed was in accordance with which he worshipped his Maker; what [+h+]is birthplace was, what his social position was; you cared nothing whether he [-xxx-] were a capitalist or wage worker, mechanic or farmer, lawyer or business man; what you wanted to know was whether when the crisis came he would "stay put."

It is just so in civil life. I believe that this country is going to rise to a pitch not merely of power but of high and true greatness such as no other country has ever shown, because I believe that our average citizen now in peace has profited and will profit by the lessons taught in the civil war by the men of '61; and that we shall apply practically the two lessons of which I have spoken; that we shall [-apply-][+show+] as a nation
[pb 11 425:0529] that what we seek is not mere ease, not mere comfort, not mere material well-being, important though that material well-being is; but that we shall try to do in our lives individually and collectively as a nation the things worth doing and try to do them well; and finally that we shall realize so far as any human power can realize, [-[?the?]-] brotherhood in fact as well as in name, and shall continue to treat this government as it was meant to be treated by those who founded it and by those who preserved it, as a government not of license, but of liberty under[+,+] by and through the law, [-of [?liberty to?] good government-], and socially and governmentally as a system under which so far as finite human ability enables us to reach that [-[illegible]-][+end+], [-a system under which-] each man is treated not with regard to the accidents of his wealth or his poverty, his occupation or his social position, but with reference to his fundamental qualities as a man among his fellows.

I thank you all for coming out to greet me. I thank the man of the Grand Army. I thank my comrades of the lesser war and the men of the national guard. For let us remember always that exactly as we pay honor to the men of the great war, so the man, regular or volunteer, regular or national guardsman, who wears the uniform that entitles him in time of emergency to fight under the flag, has a peculiar claim upon the good-will of all Americans. (Cheers and applause)
