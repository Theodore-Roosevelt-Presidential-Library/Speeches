My fellow-countrymen:

It is indeed a great pleasure to have the chance of seeing you this afternoon. I am on my way to St.Louis, to take part in commemorating the transfer a century ago of the great country on the west of the Mississippi to the United States. When the Continental Congress first declared us to be a nation the nation consisted of a string of small straggling countries stretched along the seaboard of the Atlantic; and the aggregate population of the nation was not as great as that of the state of Missouri to-day.

Our people then took up what was to be for a century, with the exception of saving the nation's life, the nation's greatest work- that was the conquest of the continent, the thrusting the frontier ever westward until there was no frontier left,until our people spread in a solid mass from the Atlantic to the Pacific. Great has been our material prosperity during the century and a quarter since we declared ourselves to be of right a free and independent nation. The world has never seen such industrial progress, such industrial change, as has been accomplished during that time. The settlement of this continent has gone on literally by leaps and by bounds, so that the change from the[+scattered+] log cabins in the heavy forests to our present condition, with our great cities and fertile well-tilled fields, has taken place in a hundredth part of the time which such changes have occupied in the history of the old world.

With the changes for good have come of necessity changes that were not good. As our life has grown more complex inevitably fresh problems have arisen and old problems have gathered new intensity. We should pay but a poor tribute to the men who founded this republic if our homage is one of the lips only. The homage that counts is the homage of deeds, the homage of action. To gather on the Fourth of July and say how great our fathers were and how great we are is not enough. It is a good thing to keep in [-[illegible]-] memory the greatness of the nation's past; but it is only a good thing if it spurs us on to make greater the nation's present. [pb 179 425:0315] The problems that face us are new. The methods by which we have to meet them are new in a sense also; but the basic principles upon which we must proceed if we are to solve them aright are as old as the decalogue and the golden {rulw|rule}. We can win out in our present national life, we can make the nation's future even greater than the nation's past, only if we set ourselves in good faith to solving those problems in accordance with the immutable laws of righteousness. Each of us is in a sense his brother's keeper; each must do what he can for him, and as a man strive to do his own work. (Cheers and applause.)
