Mr. Chairman, and you, my fellow-Americans, my fellow-citizens:

I could not deny myself the pleasure of coming here this evening to say a word or two of respect and greeting and appreciation for the memory of General Sigel, and of the cause which he typifies. It was my good fortune at the last session of Congress to sign a bill {f for|for} General Sigel's widow, which you, Mr. Bartholdt, were instrumental in passing.(Applause.) I wish this evening to bear testimony by a word to his services, and to point out what those services meant and symbolized to the republic. General Sigel was one of the foremost among the men, some of whom are present here this evening, who saved Missouri to the Union, and who fought in that great army which has left us not merely a heritage of honor, but which has left us a standard to which we must rise, for to that standard [-xx-] in war we in turn must rise in peace. There are many lessons for us to {learnfrom|learn from} what General Sigel and his fellows did in the iron days that tried men's souls. In the first place, the great lesson of brotherhood. We were thrice fortunate in the Civil War, in spite of all the sadness and the bitterness and the [-xxxxx-] heartburning, for that contest left us the right as a reunited nation to take [-xxxxxx-] pride, not merely in the valor [-xx-] shown by the men who wore the blue, but in the valor shown by the men who wore the gray.(Applause.) The very victory won by the men like General Sigel left the victors the right of claiming their share in the renown of the valient [-xxxxx-] vanquished, and so with the sons of the south the men of the north and south alike have a right to feel proud of all the deeds of prowess done on either side of the contest. Another lesson , oh, my friends and fellow-countrymen, we are here a new nation, different from any [-[illegible]-] old world nation, because we come of many different strains. [-[illegible]-] The American of to-day, the American of the past, the American of the future does not represent any one strain. In our composite nationality flows the blood of the majority of the {natuons|nations} of western Europe. In the Civil War our [pb 186 425:0318] heroes numbered men like Sigel and Sheridan, Grant, Sherman, men of different race origin; men who themselves or whose fathers had come from different lands, but who all alike were ready to pour out their blood like water in defense of the flag that symbolized their ideals.

My fellow-citizens, we can win in the fields of civic life, we can do our duty in peace as they did their duty in war, only by keeping true to the principle which they represented; only by treating every American Citizen on his worth as [-xx-] a man and disregarding all else.(Applause.) On the platform with me, there in the audience I see men wearing the button which shows that in the iron times they proved their manhood. (Applause.)Each one of you on the march or in battle were immensely concerned about certain things in regard to your comrades, and there were others about which you cared nothing. It was of small consequence {i indeed|indeed} what the way {w s|was} in which the man on your right hand or on your left worshipped his maker; it was of small consequence from what land he [-xxxxxxxxxx-] or his forefathers had come; it was of small consequence what his social position had been, or what his means; that was not what concerned you. What you wanted to know about the man who went forward beside you in battle was as to whether when the time of stress came his {bettle|mettle} would ring true.(Applause.) If it did he was your brother. You wanted to know that when the time came to move he would move in the right direction.(Applause.) You wanted [-xxxxxxx-] in other words , to judge him on his worth as a man. If he had courage, honesty, and sincerity of, purpose, stalwart and determined to battle to the end for the cause to which he had sworn fealty then you held him as [+a+] worthy comrade, and all else was as nothing in the balance compared to it. (Applause.)

In the field of social and civic effort we can win only precisely on those terms. The man who seeks to stir up one class of our citizens against another, or to divide one class of our citizens against another on any other line of cleavage save the line of cleavage which separates right from wrong does to all of us a foul injustice.(Applause.)To try to array class against class, section against section, any body of our citizens against any other body for an artificial or improper reason [pb 187 425:0319] is to do evil to the entire body politic. We can win,[-xxx-] as we will win, and make this republic during the century that has opened the brightest, the greatest, the most orderly and the freeest republic upon which the sun has ever shone,only if we approach our task to-day in the spirit with which General Sigel [-xxxx-] and his colleagues and his comrades approached their's in the Civil War; the spirit that cares nothing for the accidental differences of social position or means, the spirit that scorns to dwell upon the difference in origin , in {borth|birth} place, the spirit that says of every other American, are they true, courageous, sincere, honest, if so, I challenge them as my brother; the spirit [-thatxxxxxx-] of Abraham Lincoln, the spirit of national brotherhood in its deepest and broadest {signigicance|significance}, if we are true to that ideal we shall win out; if we are false to it we shall fail, and shame to us for the failure.

I have said that many race strains join to make American citizenship. From each strain something of value can be gotten for the good of our common country; from all of them we can get the highest citizenship on one condition, and that [-xx-] condition is the frank acceptance on our part of the underlying principle to which we cannot afford to be recreant, the principle of American citizenship, the principle of judging each man on his worth as a man, and if he does his part well, hail him as our brother in truth and in fact.(Cheers and applause.)
