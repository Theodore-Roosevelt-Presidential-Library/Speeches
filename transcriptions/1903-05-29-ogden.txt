Mr. Mayor; Senator Smoot; and you, My Fellow Citizens: Men and Women of Ogden, Utah:

It is a great pleasure to come before you this afternoon; and if I needed, which I think I do not, an indication of what was done in irrigation, I should appeal to the experience of the people who have made so marvelous a success of irrigated agriculture in this beautiful valley. (Cheers and applause) What you have succeeded in doing is {sufficientto|sufficient to} show the wisdom of trying to develop in every way the irrigated agriculture of the country. I was more pleased than I can say to have been able to render any aid whatsoever in putting upon the national statute books the law which I consider[-s-] second in beneficence to none connected with {out|our} internal development since the homestead law was passed. I am delighted that the national irrigation congress is to be held here next fall; and I congratulate the state of Utah {uponnthe|upon the} fact that its legislature was the first to pass an appropriation for such a congress.

There can be nothing of greater importance to the welfare and growth of our country during the half century that is opening than this question of irrigation. It is of vital consequence to the growth of all of the states of the Rocky Mountains and immediately to either side; a[?nd?] anything that is of such consequence to one portion of our country is necessarily of consequence to all. I cannot with too much emphasis say that every wise and patriotic man will favor any scheme for the betterment of part of the country whether it is his own section or not; because whatever helps part of us in the long run helps all. (Applause) Fundamentally we go up or go down together. Prosperity {dees|does} not stop at state lines, and neither does adversity. When prosperity comes , while it may come unequal[+ly+], yet it comes somewhat to all; and when adversity comes, while some will suffer more than others, yet all must suffer somewhat.

The greatest lesson which the American body politic needs to take to heart now at the beginning of the twentieth century is that it is out of the question permanently for our people to progress save on lines which tell for [+the+] progression of all; that you cannot raise permanently one section by depressing another, one class by depressing another; and the man is recreant to the principles of our {gove rnment|government}, no less than to the welfare of our people, who {seeksto|seeks to} arouse any
[pb 168 425:0510] feeling among Americans against their fellow Americans whether he makes his appeal in the fancied interest of one section or in the fancied interest of a class. We can go up and we shall go up only by each of us keeping in mind, not merely his own rights, but his duties to his neighbor; meaning by neighbor every man within the limits of this {bread|broad} land. The safe motto on which to act is the motto not of some men, but all men[+,+] up. (Applause) Therefore I feel that it was not merely my privilege but my duty to ask the national government, the government representing the people of the entire Union, to do all in its ;power for the furtherance of the interests of those states whose success is largely dependent upon the application of the[-xxx-] principles of {irrigaton|irrigation}. You know the proverb: "The Lord helps those who help themselves." If you throw all the duty of helping you on the Lord, he will put it back on you. It is the same way with your fellow man. Providence is not going to do everything for you and the national government cannot. All that the national government can do is to try to give you a fair show, to help you to the chance of doing your work under favorable conditions; and then the work has got to be done by you, yourself. As one step toward doing that work I most earnestly hope that you and all the other states in interest will in every way endeavor to make the meeting of the irrigation congress here in Ogden a thorough success. I mean that not merely in the interest of Ogden, not merely [-in-][+of+] the states which are to be benefitted by irrigation, but in the interest of the Union. I want to see that congress a success ; I want to see the work of irrigation the greatest possible success. (Applause)

Here in the audience to-day at Ogden I am greeted by one class of your citizens whom I feel I have the concurrence of all others in putting foremost[+,+] in giving for all time the right of the line[+[?--?]+] [-for all time to-] the men of the Grand Army. (Applause) I am also greeted by the younger men, my own comrades, who [-ashore and [illegible]-], both ashore and afloat did their duty in the war of '98. (Applause) I want to say just a word to you about them. When I greet the m[-[?a?]-][+e+]n and women [+of the generation+] that fought the civil war - for mind you, the woman who stayed at home and sent husband or lover[+ or+] father to the war, that sent the breadwinner off[?,?]and tried [+to do+] her best without him at home, knowing that he might never come back, she deserves just as much recognition as the man {whowent|who went}. (Cheers and applause) In fact when I speak of good citizenship I am just as apt to think of a woman as of a man; and in the partnership between man and woman I am not
[pb 169 425:0511] by any means sure that it is not the [-xx-] man that generally has the best of it. One thing I do know; and that is that no other citizen in the country has quite the [+same+] claim upon us as the woman who has brought up to {behonorable|be honorable} [?man?] and women her children, who has done her duty in the home to husband and to children[illegible] [-you of the civil war and you my comrades of the lesser war -- for in our case it was not so very much of a job, but we did it -- and I want to take just one lesson from what they did.-] [-[illegible]-] at Salt Lake I have spoken of the lessons to be drawn in our domestic and civic life from the conducts of the men who fought in the great civil war. We have many new problems to face without our boundaries. Many new problems have arisen and will arise as {inci dents|incidents} of [-[illegible]-][+our+] tremendous growth of our complex industrial civilization . We need to devise new methods of meeting those problems; but the spirit with which we must approach them if we are to succeed, is the spirit shown by the men who in '61 answered when Abraham Lincoln called -- (Cheers and applause) a spirit of broad humanity, broad brotherhood, a spirit of manliness which will not endure wrong and will not inflict it, both. I do not want to see you endure wrong; and I do not want to see you {inflist|inflict} it[+.+] [-and in addition [?to?] cool-headed sanity.-] If there is {oone|one} quality which we must try to eradicate from our dealings with any of the social or industrial problems which arise from time to time it is the quality of hysteria . Banish brutality envy, greed, hatred, -- banish them all and banish with them all forms of emotional hysteria. We need cool headed, sane commonsense in dealing with the problems that confront the nation, just as we {re ed|need} it in dealing {wach|each} with the problems that confront him or her in his or her own household. We need to draw a lesson from the conduct of the men of the civil war even in conducting our affairs in peace. We need also to draw a moral from their conduct as to how to handle ourselves in the great work of the world, which, whether we wish it or not, we must undertake. A nation like ours cannot play a small part. A small people, a weak people, a people with a limited territory, little wealth and few inhabitan[+t+]s can play [+a+] small part with dignity and propriety; but a big nation like ours cannot. We must play a big part[+.+]We can play it badly, or we can play it well; but play it we have got to; and as we have to I know too well the spirit of my countrymen to hesitate as to the way in which it [-[?shall?]-][+will+] be played. (Applause)

In the civil war the men who did the business did not boast more than they could make good. They did not say what they could not do. The people who called
[pb 170 425:0511] "On to Richmond! " and demanded that within three {weks|weeks} of gathering you should go to Richmond were not the people with rifles at the front. They were the people behind. The men in front knew they had quite a job on hand; they knew it would take some time and were bent on seeing it through[-[?,?]-]. The same people who at one moment would shriek for an immediate victory and triumph at Richmond[+,+] two weeks afterward when perhaps Bull Run had {oc curred|occurred}[+,+] would say that the war was a failure; that it had ended. They were in error. It did not end for three years and a {ha lf|half} afterwards, and then it {endedthe|ended the} [-xx-] other way.

In dealing with foreign affairs I {waht|want} our people to copy the attitude of the men {whoo|who} did not brag and did fight in the civil war. No good comes of [-[illegible]-] [+speaking+] insultingly of other nations. On the contrary it is a mark of {aweak|a weak} man to bluster, always. I used to live in the cow country [-[illegible]-], and we had a proverb to the effect: Do not draw unless you mean to shoot. That is pretty good sense for the nation as well as for the individual. Do [+not+] make a claim that we are not prepared[-x-] to back up. Do not talk loosely or loudly as to what we will do to other nations[+,+] in a way that will cause them to feel that we are acting in an insulting and an aggressive way. Treat them with absolute courtesy; and that having been done make up our minds what the interest and honor of America require; state it, and make it good when stated. I believe in the Monroe doctrine with all my heart and soul and I intend to see that it is made good. I believe that {out|our} {interets|interests} in the {Pacificare|Pacific are} such that we need always to be ready to protect them. You can make good the Monroe doctrine; you can protect our interests in the Pacific; you can protect them in the Atlantic; [-you [illegible]-] you can keep this nation in the position she has attained, only by going on with the building up of the United states navy. When I appeal for the navy I appeal for something which should meet a response in every American heart; for the navy is as much the concern of the man who lives in the Mississippi valley or beside the Rocky Mountains as [-[?don?]-][+of+] the man who lives on the coast of either ocean; because a victory for the navy is a victory for each and all of us; and defeat would cause each of us to hang his head. No man {thesfore|therefore} stands as more typically representative of the interests of all our people than the man whose duty it is to see to the building up and the rendering and keeping efficient of the navy of the United States so far as Congress gives him that power; and no man of recent years who has held the position of Secretary of the Navy has done {m ore|more} to render it {effici ent|efficient} than the man I am about to introduce to you -- Secretary Moody[+,+] who will now speak to you. (Cheers and applause)
