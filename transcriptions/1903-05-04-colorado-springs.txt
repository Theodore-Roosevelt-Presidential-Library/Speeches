My Friends and Fellow Citizens:

It is a great pleasure [-xxxxxxxx xxx-] to have the chance of being with you again. I know your state and I know your city well. (Applause) I am {gladto|glad to} have the chance of returning here to say a few words of greeting to you and to acknowledge your welcome. (Applause)

And let me first thank the men of the Grand Army of the Republic (Applause) for having {turnedout|turned out} to welcome me to-day. All of us owe a peculiar debt to them,for there would not be any country,nor any President to address you if it was not for what they did. And what they did must serve not merely as an example to us in time of war, but as an example to us in time of peace. For fundamentally, speaking from the standpoint of the whole, disregarding the exceptions, the same qualities that are necessary for the making of a good soldier are necessary for the making of a good citizen. (Applause) There are two sets of those qualities. In the first place we must have that spirit of decency, of honesty, of patriotism, which takes shape as love of country, as love of ones fellows, which makes a man good in the home, a good man to work with or to deal with, which gives him a spirit of devotion to the flag-- we must have all of that, and then in addition we must have the rugged manliness of quality which makes virtue and decency effective.

In the civil war the first thing that you demanded of one of your own men was that he should love his country, that he should be capable of loyalty -- loyalty to his fellows, loyalty to his organization, loyalty to the regiment, the army, the flag. If he did not have that quality in him then his courage would avail nothing, for there was nothing in that case to make the courage show itself on the right side. But that quality was not enough. No matter how patriotic the man was, it did not help if he had a tendency to run away. In addition to patriotism the man had to have the spirit that made him do, and dare, and endure; that iron will [+that urged him on+] until out of trial, out of effort, out of temporary failure, the ultimate triumph came. He had to have that spirit in him or his love of country could not avail. So it is now in civic life. As the base, as the foundation, we must have honesty, decency, the spirit of fair dealing between man and man, the spirit that makes employer and wageworker, the

[pb 59 425:0355]

dweller on the farm and the dweller in the towns, able each to understand the viewpoint of his {br other|brother}; and while resolute to see that he himself receives justice, resolute also not to try to wrong that brother. We must have that first ; but we need more than mere morality, than mere virtue. The virtue that is cloistered, the virtue that stays at home, that sits in its own parlor and wonders why the world is so bad, does not help anyone. Together with decency and honesty must go the spirit that impels a man, not merely to talk about the wrong in the world, but to go out and [-[?fight to?]-] try to make things a little better in the world. (Applause)That you have got to have first. And in that struggle to make things better we have to recollect that there is no more room in civil life than in military life for all of us to be brigadier generals. (Laughter and applause)

You remember, my comrades, in the days of the civil war how all the people that [-xx-][+were+] not in it could tell you what to do to win ; and that there were plenty of people who did not want to be privates because they thought the job was not big enough for them, and there did not seem to be an opening for them higher up. The [+victory+] was won, primarily, because together, of course, with the generalship of Grant, and Sherman, and Thomas, and Sheridan, together with the statesmanship of Lincoln, there was as the prime factor the fact that the average man in blue had the right spirit in him, that the man behind the gun had the spirit that drove him forward to victory.

[pb 60 425:0356]

We will win to a higher standard of social and civic duty only by and through the spirit that makes the average citizen wish to do well the work of {avera ge|average} citizenship, and you cannot do it by spurts. The world is pretty old now, and yet it is a long way [-xx-] from the millenium. Each victory we win as long as we live will of necessity open the way for [-the-][+a+] fresh struggle. We can win and will win only by making up our minds that life is not an easy thing, that life is a hard thing; and that the glory comes not in seeking to make it easy, but in seeking to do the hard task well; that is how the triumph comes (Applause.) You[+,+] men of the Civil War-what are the memories you have handed [-on-][+down+] to your children, and [-of-][+to+] all of us who have come after you,-the memories of your lives of which you are proud[-.-][+?+] [-[?t?]-][+T+]he times of ease, the times when there was no effort[???] No, the times when you knew what it was to walk with swollen feet and throbbing head under the midsummer sun of the south until it seemed as if you could not stay in the ranks any longer, and yet you did stay; or the times when in the ragged blanket you lay shivering in the frozen mud of the midwinter nights; shivered and shivered[+,+] and thought about home and thought about comfort, but you put through the job that you had in hand; the time when after months of weary inaction there came a battle,maybe a defeat, and those of little faith said that the war was over. Why, after the[-x-] battle of Bull Run there were [-p[illegible]xx-] people who said the war was over, and it was not over for nearly four years and then it was over the other way.(Applause.) So it must be with us here in civil life. We must approach our duties, our life, with the firm determination to put through the [-xx-] task, to do all that there is to be done to win in the end, and if defeat comes first[+,+] to set our teeth and come up to win the next time.

In greeting all of you I want to say just one word about something that impressed me so much to-day in Denver, and that I did not have a chance to speak of then. I was greeted by thousands of school children of Denver. I was very glad to see them. I want to say [-x-] just one word upon the future of the country, [-xxThxxxxxx-] the country as [+it+]

[pb 61 425:0356]

[-willxxxxx-] will be twenty, thirty, forty years hence. A good deal depends upon how we handle business, upon how we do the great industrial work, upon how we handle the farms and the ranches; but the thing that counts most, the thing that will [-x-]count most will be the kind of men and women that there are then in the country. No nation is sound if there are not in the average family plenty of healthy and happy children. Those children, if not brought up well are not merely a curse to themselves and to their parents, but[-x-] mean the ruin of the state in the future. The schools can do much, but if the homes do not do their part the schools cannot entirely make good the deficiency. The fathers and mothers have got to do their full part in making the boys and girls such that there is a reasonable chance of getting good citizenship out of the future; or they are [-[illegible]-] derelict in the most vital of all of the duties of citizenship. You can do nothing if you start with the idea that the first consideration is to save either [-you-][+yourself+] or the children from trouble or effort. There are a good many contemptible creatures in the world, but on the whole the most contemptible is the creature who wants to go through life bent only on having the easiest time that life can give, and next to him in point of folly comes the man or the woman who through weakness[+—+] for it is not real affection[+—+] tries [+not+] to make the child able to meet and to overcome difficulties, but to shirk them. That was not the way you did your work from '61 to '65. That is not the way you want to see those who come after you do their work in war or in peace. And I ask of all our people that they not only do their own duty, but that they train those who are to come after them that true happiness consists not of that most vapid of all pursuits, the pursuit of pleasure as a sufficient end in itself, but in the effort each to do his or her duty as that duty arises. (Cheers and applause.)
