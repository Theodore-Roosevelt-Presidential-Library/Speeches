Mr. Chairman; and Ladies and Gentlemen:

It is a great pleasure to me once more to be with you. During the last few days I have felt emphatically that I was in a country that I knew and among people that I knew. When I come out to the western edge of the Dakotas , [-[illegible]-] Nebraska and the states just west of them I come to a region in which for a number of years I worked and lived. I know the country and I know the people.

{Itis|It is} a liberal education for an easterner to come west or for a westerner to come east, for the great thing that we need to keep ever before [-xx-] our eyes in this country is that a good citizen is a good citizen anywhere. (Applause) I believe emphatically that the best thing that can be done in the way of getting American citizens to work together is to get them to know one another, for I feel that the average American citizen is a pretty good fellow, and all that is necessary for him is to know the other average American citizen and they will get on. There is not any royal road to good citizenship; and there is not any royal road to prosperity. Something can be done, and at times a good deal can be done, by the law, but fundamentally the qualities upon which we must rely to get decent work in [-private-][+public+] life are the qualities upon which we must rely to get decent work in private life. I am speaking now in a country of cattle growers, in a town that is preeminently a railroad center. I therefore speak to representatives of the greatest classes that go to make up American life. I speak to the farmer, the stockgrower, the merchant, the man of business, the wageworker[+.+] [-f-][+F+]undamentally what you want in the man here is to be a good neighbor, a good friend, is not especially that he should be brilliant, not especially that he should have wonderful genius, but that he should possess the ordinary, humdrum, everyday qualities of decency, of honesty, of courage, and of common sense. What you prize in a man with whom you {ded|deal} in a business way, in a man whose store is next to yours, or whose farm joins yours, is that when he gives his word he should keep it; that he should be able to do his own work, and that while doing his own business well he should not interfere improperly with you; that he can help himself and at need join with you for the common good. It is just so in public life. A promise is as sacred if made on the stump as if made off the stump. A promise is as sacred if made by a public man in a public speech as if made by a private man to another private man and [pb 120 425:0285] it must be kept in one case as in the other. There is a converse [?side?] to that. Always keep your promises. On the other hand never ask for the making of a promise which you ought to know cannot be kept. If you deal with a man, or a storekeeper, and he promises you the impossible, on the one hand he is a knave to make the promise, and on the other hand you are none too wise to believe him. If he says he will give you five dollars worth of goods for $2.50, you ought to know that he cannot fulfil his promise, for he cannot carry on his business on that basis. You cannot afford permanently to try to get on with a man who will tell you that he will do what he cannot do, and you cannot afford to demand that he should [-[?tell you?]-] promise you what he cannot do. So it is in public life . If any man tells you that by any law he can bring about the millenium, distrust him. The millenium is a good way off yet. What he can promise is this: that he will do his best to get such laws that under them the average man can work to the best advantage and use his faculties to the best advantage, and he can further promise that the law when passed shall be executed with absolute honesty and fairness, with justice to all alike, rich or poor, with absolute indifference. Those promises can be made [-[illegible]-], and those promises can and must be kept. But something can be done by law, sometimes a good deal can be done by the law, and even more can be done by the honest and fearless administration of the law. But after all, in the last resort, the chief factor in any man's success must be the sum of tha[-[illegible]-][+t+] man 's own qualities. Nothing can take the place of the man's individual hardihood, resolution, thrift and energy. No law has ever been devised which will make a coward brave, or a fool wise, or a weakling strong. All that the law can do is to so shape conditions that the man who is honest, who has courage, who has physical and mental capacity, can [?use?] those gifts best to his own advantage without harming his fellows. Something [?can?] be done by each [-[illegible]-][+[illegible]+] for everyone else. There is not one of us here to-day who does not at times slip, who does not at times need a helping hand to be stretched out to him or her, and in such cases woe to the man who says[+,+]I am not my brother's keeper; woe to the man who does not help stretch out that hand and help another as he will surely himself at some time wish to be helped. Help [-[illegible]-][+the+] man if he stumbles, but if he insists on lying down, you cannot carry him; help him up, start him to walk, but if he will not walk you cannot carry him as well as yourself. {Youcan|You can} do your best to aid him ; you can help him in the only way in which it is worth while trying to help any man; you can help him to help [pb 121 425:0286] himself, but that is all you or any man can do. That can be done, and that each of us is in honor bound to try to {dp|do}.

The qualities that make a good neighbor, a man worth knowing as a friend in private life, are fundamentally the same that you need in dealing with the affair[-[?i?]-]s of state. In the first place honesty, decency, --I use the word in its widest sense. I mean fair dealing toward all; [-[illegible]-] the honesty that looks a man straight in the face without fear; the quality that makes a man a good husband, a good father, decent in the family, decent in relations with those [-[?with?]-] whom he has under him, or whom he serves, or with whom he does business. You need honesty first as a foundation, and that is not enough. Out here on the plains the men who built up this country, not only had to be good men, but they had to be men. They had to have the strong and hardy qualities; they had to have courage, hardihood, the spirit that endures and dares. They had to have that, or they made but a poor fist of the work. In addition to honesty you have got to have courage, you have got to have strength, you must have strong virile qualities that we associate with the word man when we say of a man he is one indeed. And that is not enough. Not only must you have courage and honesty but something more, for no matter how brave and how honest a man is if he is a fool you can do nothing with him. You must have courage and honesty and joined with them common sense, intelligence, the power to plan, the sense to try to foresee, the sense to try to do the best with the implements at hand, to get the utmost possible , and not to wreck one[+'+]s self by striving after the impossible. That is what we need in citizenship; and good citizenship is the chief and best product that one can produce in a state.

I congratulate Nebraska upon all that it produces. I congratulate it upon its share in the splendid prosperity that the whole country has enjoyed; upon the hardihood and intelligence with which it is proceeding to work; but most of all I congratulate Nebraska upon its men and its women. I believe in you and your future, because I know {yuu|you}. I am especially glad to see the children. After all, they are the best product. I have been pleased at meeting them. I believe in the stock and I want to see it go on.

In closing let me thank you once more for coming here to greet you, and say with all my heart I appreciate it; that I am glad to see you and that I wish well now and hereafter to you and yours. (Cheer and applause)
