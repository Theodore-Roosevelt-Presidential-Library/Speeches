Mr. Chairman, Mr. Gudger, and you, my fellow Americans: It is indeed a pleasure to have the chance of saying a word of greeting to you this morning, to speak here in the midst of your beautiful country of western North Carolina, in this beautiful city in front of the monument raised to Senator Vance (Applause), and to be addressing people whose forefathers have borne a distinguished part in every great military or civic struggle through which this country has come (Applause). It was not far from here, as we measure distance in America, that the Mecklenburg Declaration of Independence was formulated (Applause). The gentleman who introduced me spoke of the great deeds of the men who in the Revolutionary War followed Marion and McDowell. My forefathers fought under Marion (Applause); my forefathers fought with the Georgian and South Carolinian troops who battled throughout the dark days when [-xxx-] Cornwallis and the red dragoons of Tarleton overran the southern states. They were present at Kings Mountain, at the Cowpens, and they saw the final triumph when the men in blue and buff who followed Greene out of defeat wrested victory, and when at last the flag of the thirteen United States, who had been the Thirteen Colonies, waved without a rival along the sea coast and along the foot hills of the mountains (Applause).

It has been a great and peculiar pleasure to be greeted as I was to-day by the men who served in the Confederate army. Yesterday and to-day I traveled through a region which sent its sons, some to wear the blue, some to wear the gray , all to serve with courage and self-devotion the right as it was given to each to see the right. The day before yesterday I went over the battlefields of Chickamauga and [-x-] Chattanooga - over that space of territory which saw for two months one of the gigantic death wrestles of the civil war - the territory partly in Tennessee, partly in my mother's state of Georgia (Applause); and I feel that the man would be but a poor American who did not come from the scenes commemorating the valiant deeds of those armies a better American than when he started. While I was there a delegation of young men from the state of Georgia came [pb 46 424:0705] to present me a cane cut from the battlefield, with the names of three Union and three Confederate generals on it. One of the Union generals, General Boynton, was showing me around the field. One of the Confederate generals, General Joe Wheeler, had been my chief in the Spanish war (Applause). Yesterday we stopped at a little station in Tennesee, and among those who gathered to greet me was an old fellow who had worn the gray. He said- "I was one of Wheeler's boys". I said "So was I" (Laughter and applause). It is a good thing for an American president to have the chance to travel through different sections of the country , because it is a mighty good thing for any American to meet his fellow Americans of different parts of the country in order that he may realize how trivial are the points of unlikeness and how essential are the points of likeness (Applause ). A good American is a good American, and I don[+'t+] care a snap of my finger whether he comes from the north or the south or the east or the west . If he is a straight and decent man I am for him, and if he isn't I am not (Applause). Oh, my friends, the lesson of brotherhood, the lesson that is taught by such a greeting as I am receiving at this moment, the lesson that is taught wherever you see valiant and true men who wore the blue meet valiant and true men who wore the gray and strike hands with them - that lesson applies through all our national life, and it applies just as much in forming a judgment between class and class as between section and section. We never can succeed in making this country what it can and shall be made until we work together, not primarily as northerners or southerners, easterners or westerners, not primarily as employe[-d-][+r+] or employed, townsmen or countrymen, capitalists or wage workers - but primarily as American citizens (Applause), to whom the right of brotherly friendship and comradeship with all other decent American citizens comes as the first and greatest of privileges.

I wish to thank the men of the State Guard who have turned out as my escort; and I want to draw [-[illegible]-]{o ne|one} lesson for civic life from the deeds of the men of the civil war, from the experiences that you and I of the younger generation have had. At Chickamauga, at Chattanooga, in all the fighting from '61 to '65, the fighting in which you, Mr. Mayor, and those like you won such imperishable renown, the men who followed Grant [pb 47 424:0705] and the men who followed Lee, were differently armed from the way in which their forefathers who followed Washington, Greene, Wayne, and Marion were armed; they were differently uniformed; they were drilled by different tactics; but the spirit that drove them on was the same (Applause). And now you of my generation, you of the National Guard, whose friends and brothers served with or beside me in the Spanish war, if ever there should be need - and I am thankful to say I think that the chance is but small - of going to war for the country again, we shall fight with different weapons, we shall need the small caliber, high-power, smokeless rifle, we shall fight in open order instead of according to the old touch of elbow tactics - but we shall win only if we have the spirit that drove you to victory (Applause). Now we need good weapons. I never see, either in my own state or in North Carolina or anywhere else, the National Guard armed with black powder muskets without feeling indignant. Upon my word, it is like giving us crossbows. I feel that a great and rich people should be able to give the best type of weapon to the men who wear the uniform (Applause). You need a good weapon. If you have a bad weapon and you are pitted against a good man you will come out second best. But if you have the best weapon in the world and are a poor creature yourself, you will be beaten by a good man with a club (LAughter and applause). We need the gun, and we need even more the man behind the gun.

[-[?Well gentlemen,?]-] it is just the same [-[?thing?]-] in the endless battle for civic right, civic decency, which must go on in time of peace. We need good laws, we need [-[illegible]-] honest and upright administration of the laws, but we need as the fundamental prerequisite for good government a high average standard of citizenship in the men who make the laws and stand back of them (Applause). The best law by itself will not bring good citizenship. A bad law will do incalculable harm, and no greater damage can be done to any community than maladministration, dishonest or unfair administration, of the laws. A law badly administered can do great harm, and often most harm to those who at any given moment may think that it is to their interest to have the law violated or to have it put upon the statute books. Bad [pb 48 424:0706] laws can do harm. Good laws can do a measure of good; but it is the man behind the law who will ultimately by the way in which he performs his duties determine whether this country is to go up or down (Applause). In the last analysis the problem of getting good government is the old, old problem of getting good men and good women as citizens of the state. There is not any patent device for getting good citizenship. There are countries with constitutions like ours, with laws not unlike ours, where, nevertheless, the experiment of free government has been well-nigh a failure, because they did not have the same material upon which to build. We need good citizenship, and in making good citizenship brilliancy and intellect are good things, but there is something a thousandfold more important, and that is character (Applause). Character is what counts. The men here who fought in the Union army[+,+] the men who fought in the Confederate army - let them look back. What did they care for most in the men who were their comrades, who fought on their right hand and their left hand in time of action? It was not as to the man's antecedents, it was not as to whether he was rich or poor, or what his social position had been -- it was to know whether when the time of crisis came he would "stay put" (Laughter and applause)[+.+] [-[illegible]-] if the need came to go forward you wanted to be dead sure when he moved that he would move in the right direction. You wanted to know that you could count on his being there as a good friend and as a foe to look out for. You wanted to have the man devoted to his country, devoted to his flag, loyal to his regiment; but I don't care how fine his feelings were, if he ran away he was no good (Laughter). It is so in civil life, for if a citizen is to be worth his salt he needs character, and into character many elements enter, but above all these three: In the first place, honesty - decency. If a man isn't decent, then the abler he is the more dangerous he is to the community. In the Revolutionary War one of the bravest and most brilliant soldiers during the early years of the contest [-is-][+was+] the man who has left his name as a by-word of infamy to the nations for all time - the man who fought with distinguished gallantry in Canada, the man who led all the American forces in the great decisive battle at Saratoga and left his leg [pb 49 424:0706] on the field of victory[+.+] that man with all [-x-] his courage, all his daring, all his {su perb|superb} military genius, turned because the root of righteousness was not in him - sought to betray his comrades for money, and left the name of Benedict Arnold as a hissing for evermore. In civil life the danger is not so patent, but it is just as great, If ability is not accompanied by a rightful sense of accountability to the moral law . You must have honesty, decency; but decency and honesty are not enough. I don't care how good a man is, if he is a timid man you can do but little with him. In addition to honesty and decency, you must have courage. I want to see every one a good man, and in addition to that I want to see him a man. We must have the manly virtues deeply imbedded as part of our national characteristics if we are to do our work aright in peace or in war. But honesty and courage together are not enough. I care not how brave and how honest a man is, if he was born foolish you can do but little with him (Laughter and applause). We need honesty, we need courage, and we need in addition the saving grace of common sense.

Gentlemen, I did not come here to preach to you, though you may think I [-[illegible]-][+[?do;?]+] I c[+[?a?]+]me here[-xx-] merely to give utterance to the beliefs that you and I have deep down in our hearts. We can make this republic what it will surely be made only on condition of practicing the old, old every-day, commonplace virtues which have made up good citizenship from the time of the first republics of Greece to the present day. There is not any patent recipe for being a good soldier now. You will be a good soldier now if you have the stuff in you that made good soldiers in the days of Grant and Lee, that made good soldiers in the days of Washington, that made good soldiers from the time that history dawned. If you have not got the stuff in you there will be difficulty in getting it out of you (Laughter and applause). And so in citizenship; there is no patent {de- device|device} by which we can make this country prosper; there is no patent device by which we can make the average citizen a credit to the country - make him hold higher the standard of good government. The average citizen must make himself a worthy citizen of the republic. The average citizen must realize that it is upon his shoulders that the entire government structure rests. We get in the habit of speaking of the government as if [pb 50 424:0707] it were something apart from us. Now the government is us - we are the government, you and I (Applause); and the government is going to do well or ill accordingly as we with sanity, with resolution, with broad charity and sound common sense make up our minds that the affairs of the government shall be managed. No man can take the burden of government off the {shpulders|shoulders} of a people fitted for self-government. No man can make it easy for any people to govern themselves. They have got to determine for themselves [-xxx-] what the government shall be, and in the long run the government will represent their wishes - their wishes for good or their wishes for evil, or else they are indifferent to good or evil, [-[illegible]-] (Applause). And so my plea to you, my fellow {Amrigans|Americans}, as to every citizen in every section of the country, is, remember that in this country nothing - no law, no leadership - can possibly take the place of the exercise by the average citizen of the fundamental virtues of good citizenship - the exercise of the fundamental qualities of honesty, courage, and common sense (Great applause).
