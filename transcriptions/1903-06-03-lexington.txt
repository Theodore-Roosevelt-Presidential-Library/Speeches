My Fellow Citizens:

It is a great pleasure to be here this afternoon and have the chance of greeting you. I have been traveling through your great state all to-day. I know it of old. Whenever I come within its borders I am impressed as all men must be by the wonderful wealth of resources that you have . Agriculturally, industrially, commercially, this state is bound to [-[?state?]-][+stand+] always as it now [-[?is?]-][+does+], among the two or three greatest in the Union; and [-[?this?]-][+it+] [-[?state?]-] has shown to a preeminent degree that[+,+] ample though its material resources are, and while its sons know how to take advantage of those resources, it yet appreciates to the [-xxxx-] full that the material is but one side, that though material well-being is absolutely indispensable as the foundation for a state[+,+] as for an individual, it is but the foundation.

Wherever I go in Illinois I am met by men wearing the button of the Grand Army of the Republic, by men who show thereby that when the nation called for the highest sacrifice that man can make they responded gladly to the call. To the men of the great war we owe the fact that to-day there is a common country and that an American President can travel across this continent, from one ocean to the other, and everywhere be at home among his fellow Americans. ({A pplause|Applause}) You taught us lessons not only of war, but to be applied in peace. You taught us what are the essential and what the non-essential things. Among the non-essential things is the life of ease, the life of mere comfort, the life spent merely in avoiding trouble, avoiding risk. When Abraham Lincoln called to arms in '61 the easy thing to do was to disregard it, and if you had done that we would not be here as a nation to-day. You took the better part, because you took the harder part; you trod the stony path of duty; you took your part in the greatest and most terrible four years' war of modern times, and thereby purchased for all of us, for the generations to come hereafter, perpetual peace within our own borders. You not only have left to your children and children's children a heritage of honor; you have left it to America and to mankind; and while this nation lives your deeds shall be spoken of with gratitude and admiration. That is what the men of the civil war won, because when the time came to choose between what was easy and wrong and what was hard and right [pb 41 425:0544] {t h ey|they} chose what was hard and right. In civil life it is just the same. I would preach to no man the doctrine of the easy life. The man who spends his time in seeing how he can get along and do least is not worth much in the community. The man that counts is the man that works; The man who finds what is worth doing and then does it well. No kind of mere pleasure sought for pleasure's sake can in the long run give the stern joy that comes to whoever has done difficult duty and done it well. It does not make any difference in which [+field+] that duty is done, if the work is honorable and useful and is well done, the man doing it well is a good citizen and entitled to credit from all other good citizens. It is the way the work is done that counts; not the kind of work. Just the same as in the Grand Army, every man that wears the button , whether he was a {lieute nant|lieutenant} general or whether he was an eighteen year old recruit, it did not make any difference[+,+] if he did his duty squarely he is a brother to every other decent man who wears the button. (Applause)

It ought to be just so and must be just so in our own citizenship. We cannot[+,+] if we are to make a success of this republic, draw a line of division on artificial grounds among our people. It is utterly unimportant whether the man is {emloyer|employer} or employee, farmer, lawyer, banker, or {wagewor ker|wageworker}, if he is a square man; if he is, he is a good citizen; and if he is not, he is a bad citizen, no matter what his occupation, no matter what his means. If we are wise in drawing the {lime|line} of c[+i+]tizenship we shall draw it always on the line of conduct and never on the line of occupation, locality, creed or class. The only [+way+] we can make of this {govenment|government} the success it shall be made is to act in accordance with the {spitit|spirit} of those who {prserved|preserved} the country in the civil war, the spirit of [?true?] brotherhood and of judging each man on his merit as a man. In the war you were greatly concerned about the type of man on your right hand and your left hand in battle. It is a very {comforta ble|comfortable} thing to know you have the right type of man beside you. I found that out even in small battles. You {wese|were} not in the least concerned as to what his wealth was, or his social position, you did not care whether he was a {banke r|banker} or bricklayer; you did not care what the creed was in accordance with which he worshipped his Maker; what you wanted to know was whether when the crisis came he would ""stay put." It is just the same way in civil life. What we want to know is[+,+] when the time of trial comes[+,+] if the man [+is+] going to pull his own weight, if he is a good man to work with and deal with. If he is, he is all right; otherwise he is all wrong. (Cheers and applause)
